## Abstract

Integrated Information Decomposition with minimum-mutual-information (MMI) redundancy underlies most fMRI synergy reports. Varley (2024) showed that the MMI synergy of two independent autocorrelated processes equals the self-information of the less self-predictive of the two. Which atoms carry it, how the synergy atom sts depends on lag-1 autocorrelation r₁ and lag-0 correlation q, and how much of an sts change is autocorrelation change were open. We derive the sixteen Gaussian-MMI atoms of a bivariate AR(1) pair, map sts over (r₁, q), and compare MMI with CCS redundancy, on within-subject fMRI data (14 volunteers, DMT and placebo) in a pre/post difference-in-differences (DiD). A residual diagnostic predicts each pair's sts from its measured autocorrelations and correlation. The study began as a pre-specified test of synergy up-regulation. On the family sts = −ln(1 − r₁²) + ½ ln(1 − r₁²q²) = xtx + yty + rtr; |∂sts/∂r₁| exceeds |∂sts/∂q| 32-fold at BOLD pairs' operating point. On the placebo baseline regional MMI-sts follows regional r₁, as predicted (r = 0.863 over 115 regions; cortical Spearman 0.771, spin p < 0.0001). Under DMT whole-brain MMI-sts fell (DiD −0.0809 nats, p = 0.0038), as did mean r₁ (−0.0146, p = 0.0106), correlating at r = 0.953 per subject. The diagnostic's prediction gives the sign and 114 % of the magnitude of the sts change; a finite-sample null accounts for a third to two-thirds of the residual DiD (+0.0037–0.0077 of +0.0115, p = 0.042; every null value inside its CI). CCS-sts does not track r₁ as MMI-sts does (across pairs within a window, |r| < 0.02 against +0.7), and rose under DMT (exploratory). On autocorrelated fMRI, MMI-sts contrasts between states or groups are dominated by lag-1 autocorrelation change wherever r₁ differs and lagged interaction does not change comparably. Report r₁ beside sts, run the diagnostic, consider CCS or longer lags.

## Author summary

Functional brain imaging asks how regions carry information together, not only which regions are active. A widely used measure, from integrated information decomposition, is synergy: information a pair of regions holds jointly and neither holds alone. Synergy has been reported to differ across the cortex and to pick out regions whose integration falls under anaesthesia and in disorders of consciousness, and is read as a mark of integrative processing. We show that, with the estimator almost all of these studies use, the synergy value mostly reflects something simpler: how smooth each region's signal is over time (its lag-1 autocorrelation). We derive this on a solvable mathematical family and confirm it on functional MRI data from fourteen volunteers scanned under the psychedelic DMT and under placebo. Under DMT the measured synergy fell, but so did the smoothness of the signals, and the apparent fall in synergy was predicted, in sign and size, by the fall in autocorrelation. We had set out to test the opposite hypothesis, that DMT raises synergy, and we report its refutation and what we found instead. We recommend that studies report autocorrelation beside synergy and run the diagnostic we provide, which says how much of a synergy contrast autocorrelation accounts for.

## Introduction

Integrated Information Decomposition (ΦID; Mediano et al., 2021, 2025) extends partial information decomposition (Williams & Beer, 2010) to the time-delayed mutual information (TDMI) between the past and the future of two variables, splitting it into sixteen atoms that describe how each kind of information in the past (redundant, unique to either source, synergistic) becomes each kind in the future. In fMRI the atom that has drawn most attention is synergy-to-synergy, sts, read as the information that only the joint past of two regions carries about their joint future. On resting-state BOLD it separates a redundancy-dominated sensory core from synergy-dominated association cortex (Luppi et al., 2022). Regions ranked by synergy form a "synergistic workspace" whose integrated information is reduced under propofol and in disorders of consciousness (Luppi et al., 2024). Group differences in the synergy and redundancy atoms have been reported in Alzheimer's disease, schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental conditions (Down et al., 2026; Nago et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). The studies whose settings could be read (S3 Text) use the Gaussian estimator with the MMI redundancy function (Barrett, 2015; Mediano et al., 2021) at a single lag — one TR where the lag is stated (Luppi et al., 2023; Down et al., 2026) — on BOLD series whose lag-1 autocorrelation is high because of haemodynamic smoothing and band-pass filtering.

That MMI synergy and autocorrelation are entangled is known. Barrett (2015) exhibited positive net synergy — hence positive MMI synergy, since MMI redundancy is non-negative — in dynamical Gaussian examples in which a target's own immediate past is one of the sources (his Example 1, Fig. 4a), and noted that it vanishes when the infinite pasts are the sources. Varley (2024) showed, for the aggregate forward synergy of a bivariate process (in ΦID terms str + stx + sty + sts), that MMI cannot distinguish a synergistic pair from two independent autocorrelated processes. For a pair "disintegrated" while keeping each process's autocorrelation, the MMI synergy equals the self-information of the less self-predictive of the two (his Eq. 12, derived from the additivity of mutual information under independence and the MMI maximum, so general rather than discrete-only), and the change on disintegration depends on the pair's instantaneous mutual information (Eq. 13). On 1,000 region pairs of one human subject the synergy change correlated with pairwise mutual information at r = 0.97. He writes, "speaking just as a single author", that MMI "should probably not continue to be used in its current form" (Varley, 2024). Faes et al. (2025) state the general problem — PID applied to time series rests on an implicit i.i.d. assumption that is 'typically not tested in practice and is often violated', temporal correlations have 'a profound impact' on the lag-zero decomposition, and lagged PIDs on ad hoc variables cover only 'specific portions of the system dynamics' — and propose a rate-based decomposition as the remedy. The ΦID authors have moved towards null-model normalisation of atoms (Liardi et al., 2025) and, in some studies, towards CCS redundancy (Luppi et al., 2023). Their own worked example is a coupled AR pair (Mediano et al., 2021, Fig. 5 and Appendix VI: x_{t+1} = 0.4 (x_t + y_t) + ε, y_{t+1} = 0.4 (x_t + y_t) + η, with ΦWMS — the whole-minus-sum integrated information TDMI − I(X;X′) − I(Y;Y′), to which ΦR adds rtr — and ΦR plotted against the innovation correlation, the correlation between the two noise terms), which is the symmetric coupled family of Methods at self- and cross-coefficient 0.4; the diagonal family used for the closed form is its c = 0 case. What these do not give is an account at the level of the atoms actually reported: which of the sixteen atoms carry the self-information Varley identifies, and how sts, as opposed to the aggregate synergy, depends on the two quantities a pair of BOLD series has, its lag-1 autocorrelation r₁ and its lag-0 cross-correlation q. Nor do they say where real region pairs sit on that dependence, whether the property is shared by CCS, or, for an observed sts change on real data, how much of it is autocorrelation change and how the remainder can be tested per pair rather than by a global null.

This study did not set out to provide these. It was pre-specified as a confirmatory test of whether whole-brain synergy is up-regulated under N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), a state of intensified conscious content, against the workspace account's prediction that synergy indexes conscious level. Synergy decreased. An adversarial review of the full sixteen-atom table then showed that the decrease was carried by the quantity that carries the level, the pair's within-region self-prediction, and that it was reproduced by the lag-1 autocorrelation contrast; the closed form of Methods was derived after that observation, to account for it. The question became what the estimator measures. We give the closed form, the identity sts = xtx + yty + rtr on the AR(1) family, the (r₁, q) map with the derivative ratio and the operating point of real pairs, and the same map with lagged coupling. We give the CCS comparison under the published definition, the lag dependence, the residual diagnostic with its finite-sample null, and the DMT contrast as the test case, with the sign violation and the remainder that the data add. The original pre-specified analysis and its results are in the Supporting information (S1 Text).

### Estimator

ΦID atoms were computed with `phyid` (Imperial-MIND-lab, 2026; the Python implementation of ΦID, https://github.com/Imperial-MIND-lab/integrated-info-decomp, installed from git at commit 6c5f2e9d33c985efbdf875d45cb5a2a6a5cdbf44, as pinned in `requirements.lock.txt`), `calc_PhiID(kind='gaussian', tau=1)` with `redundancy='MMI'` or `'CCS'`: a Gaussian model of the four-vector (x_t, y_t, x_{t+1}, y_{t+1}), sixteen local atoms per time point, in nats; whole-brain values are means over the 6,555 pairs. **Windowed (W = 60, the window length in TRs)**: each run cut into 14 non-overlapping 60-TR windows, each an independent fit on that window's samples alone; W = 30 (28 windows) as a second windowed estimator. **Global fit**: one Gaussian fitted to the full run, local atoms averaged into the 28 rating bins. A "cell" in this paper is one variant × estimator combination (`ts_gsr` or `ts_demean` at W = 60, at W = 30 or at the global fit); the bias simulations use the word for one simulated setting and the finite-sample null of the residual for one run × period combination, as stated where each is described. By Bartlett's variance formula the effective sample size of a correlation between two series with these autocorrelations is n / (1 + 2 Σ_k ρ_k²) (Bartlett, 1935; Afyouni et al., 2019); over the six lags of the pooled placebo function (0.868, 0.539, 0.172, −0.085, −0.174, −0.145) the denominator is 3.3, so a 60-TR window holds about 18 effective samples for each 4 × 4 fit. The finite-sample consequences are characterised below (Bias simulations) and in Results 6. The analytic work used a closed-form implementation of the same estimator (`notes/rev_phiid_fast.py`): the time mean of `phyid`'s local Gaussian mutual information equals the plug-in log-determinant mutual information and the lattice inversion is linear, so window-mean atoms are closed-form functions of the window's 4 × 4 correlation matrix. This holds for MMI, whose redundancies are minima of window-mean mutual informations; the same module evaluates the CCS atoms sample by sample from the local mutual informations, because the CCS sign masks are applied per sample (`atoms_ccs`), so the closed form serves the map, the family and the diagnostic, all MMI, and CCS is computed pointwise. It reproduces `phyid`'s window means and global-fit bin means to ≤ 2.1 × 10⁻¹⁴ and its TR-local series to ≤ 1.1 × 10⁻¹³ on every saved output (`notes/review_results/logs/phiid_fast_validate.log`, `phiid_fast_validate_local.log`). Software: Python 3.12.3 with NumPy 2.5.3, SciPy 1.18.1 and Matplotlib 3.11.1 (every package version is pinned in `requirements.lock.txt`), run single-core on a 64-bit Linux workstation (Ubuntu 24.04.3 LTS); `phyid` was the only dependency installed from a git commit.

### The two redundancy functions and the CCS definition check

Under MMI each single-target redundancy is the smaller of the two single-source mutual informations (Barrett, 2015) and the double redundancy rtr is the smallest of the four single-source, single-target mutual informations (Mediano et al., 2021, Appendix, Definition 2). Under CCS (Ince, 2017) a single-target redundancy is the local co-information at the samples where the constituent local mutual informations and the co-information share a sign, and zero elsewhere. The ΦID double redundancy under CCS is defined by Mediano et al. (2021, Appendix, Definition 1; 2025, SI Appendix, Definition 2) as the pointwise double co-information c(x; y), summed over the samples at which all marginal pointwise mutual informations and the pointwise full mutual information i(x; y) share a sign. The double co-information is the signed sum, an inclusion–exclusion over the lattice, of the local mutual informations at the fifteen nodes of the product lattice other than its bottom node rtr (the sixteen atom positions, formed as the product of the past and future redundancy lattices). `phyid` implements a different mask: it keeps c at the samples where the four single-source, single-target local mutual informations and c itself share a sign, so its fifth sign condition is that of the double co-information rather than of the full mutual information. On the lattice c ≡ rtr − sts (deviation ≤ 3 × 10⁻¹⁵; the SI Appendix of Mediano et al., 2025, states the same identity, c(x; y) = I∂^{{1}{2}→{1}{2}} − I∂^{{12}→{12}}), so under either mask CCS-sts is 0 at the selected samples and −c elsewhere, and its whole-brain value is the average of that quantity over all samples. An earlier check (`notes/partB2_ccs.md`) had verified `phyid`'s single-target CCS against an independent implementation of Ince's construction (agreement 2.1 × 10⁻¹³ over 200 random draws of the real data; 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ on a 56-sample binary hand case) and its double redundancy against the code's own algebra only. The MATLAB reference `phyid` pins (pmediano/PhiID, commit a633cc1) implements MMI only, so no reference implementation of the double redundancy was available. In the revision after the second review Definition 1 was implemented directly (`notes/partB6_ccs_definition.py`) and run beside `phyid`'s mask on the same samples, at W = 60 and the global fit, both variants. The reading was fixed in the record before the run: the marginal pointwise mutual informations are the four between one past and one future variable, and the full pointwise mutual information is that of the joint past and joint future. The rule, fixed in advance, was that the two "agree on these data" if every whole-brain CCS-sts level and primary difference-in-differences (DiD; The DMT contrast, below) differs by less than 0.001 nats and no sign-flip p crosses 0.05, and that otherwise every CCS number in the paper is the published-definition value. They differed (Results 1); all CCS values in this paper are under the published definition, with `phyid`'s beside them in `notes/review_results/partB/ccs_pub_tables.md`. The journal version (Mediano et al., 2025, SI Appendix, Definition 2) retains the definition verbatim on the sign condition, the samples "for which all marginal pointwise mutual informations, as well as the pointwise full mutual information I(X; Y), have the same sign" (checked 15 September 2026). CCS is computed here, as in `phyid`, with respect to the fitted Gaussian of the four-vector, p(x, y), which is what the SI Appendix of Mediano et al. (2025) states for their own results. For two Gaussian sources this is also Ince's published construction: the constraints of his maximum-entropy distribution P̂ (Ince, 2017, Definition 2 — both source–target marginals and the joint source marginal) fix every entry of the covariance, so P̂ is the fitted Gaussian and no projection arises; the conditionally independent distribution of his earlier construction (Ince, 2017, Definition 3) would differ and is not used.

### Closed-form atoms of a bivariate AR(1) pair

Let x_t = a x_{t−1} + ε_t and y_t = a y_{t−1} + η_t be unit-variance stationary AR(1) processes with corr(ε_t, η_t) = q, so that corr(x_t, y_t) = q, corr(x_t, x_{t+1}) = a and corr(x_t, y_{t+1}) = aq. The correlation matrix of (x_t, y_t, x_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) is S₄ = [[1, q, a, aq], [q, 1, aq, a], [a, aq, 1, q], [aq, a, q, 1]], and every Gaussian mutual information is half the log-ratio of block determinants. Write S = −½ ln(1 − a²) and C = −½ ln(1 − a²q²). Then I(x_t; x_{t+1}) = I(y_t; y_{t+1}) = S and I(x_t; y_{t+1}) = I(y_t; x_{t+1}) = C. The (x_t, x_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) block has determinant (1 − a²)(1 − q²), so I(x_t; x_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) = S and likewise I(x_t, y_t; x_{t+1}) = S. And det S₄ = (1 − q²)²(1 − a²)², the conditional covariance of the future given the past being the innovation covariance, so the time-delayed mutual information TDMI = 2S, independent of q. MMI sets the four single-source, single-target redundancies to C, the two joint-target ones to S, and rtr = C. Inverting the lattice by the Möbius inversion of Mediano et al. (2021), which defines the atoms as the solution of a linear system and imposes no sign constraint (under MMI the double redundancy is monotonic but not totally monotonic on the double-redundancy lattice, so atoms can be negative: Mediano et al., 2025, SI Appendix, Sec. III.A; Luppi et al., 2026; under MMI only the redundancy and synergy atoms are guaranteed non-negative, Down et al., 2026): rtx = rty = xtr = ytr = xty = ytx = 0; xtx = yty = rts = str = S − C; xts = yts = stx = sty = −(S − C); and

sts = 2S − C = −ln(1 − a²) + ½ ln(1 − a²q²).

Hence sts − (xtx + yty) = C = rtr, exact for all (a, q); the excess rtr vanishes at q = 0 and grows with |q|; rtr + sts = TDMI; ΦR = TDMI − I(x;x′) − I(y;y′) + rtr = C = rtr; and ∂sts/∂a = 2a/(1 − a²) − aq²/(1 − a²q²), ∂sts/∂q = −a²q/(1 − a²q²). Varley's identity is the aggregate of these: str + stx + sty + sts = (S − C) − 2(S − C) + (2S − C) = S for all q, the self-information of one process. The atom-level statement is that MMI puts that self-information into sts and rts + str, with the four "mirror" atoms xts, yts, stx, sty carrying −(S − C) each so that the sixteen sum to 2S. The family has the property corr(y_t, x_{t+1}) = corr(y_t, x_t) corr(x_t, x_{t+1}): the other region carries no information about a region's future beyond the region's own past, every cross-prediction atom is zero, and the pair's entire TDMI is self-prediction, of which MMI assigns all but the cross-lag mutual information C to sts. The identities were checked numerically to 4 × 10⁻¹⁵ over 2,000 seeded draws of (a, q) (`notes/review_results/partB/family_checks.log`). For unequal coefficients a_x ≠ a_y (the two regions' lag-1 autocorrelations) the same pipeline evaluates the matrix with cross-lag entries a_y q and a_x q numerically; there ΦR − rtr is not zero.

**The family with lagged coupling.** The AR(1) family has no lagged interaction by construction, so its statements about q are statements about instantaneous correlation only. To see what interaction does, the symmetric VAR(1) pair x_{t+1} = a x_t + c y_t + ε, y_{t+1} = a y_t + c x_t + η, corr(ε, η) = q_ε, was evaluated in closed form (stationary covariance from the discrete Lyapunov equation; `notes/partB8_coupling_map.py`, `notes/review_results/partB/coupling_map_tables.md`). a and q_ε were re-solved for each c so that the pair's r₁ and q stay at the operating point. This was added in the revision after the second review, with no prediction recorded (record, "Part B, items 6–8"). At (0.85, 0.25), sts changes with c at fixed (r₁, q) with slope −1.77 nats per unit c and curvature +40 nats per unit c². The six cross-prediction atoms stay at zero for every c in this symmetric family. sts − (xtx + yty) equals rtr only at c = 0; for c > 0 it falls below rtr (at c = +0.02, sts − (xtx + yty) = +0.005 while rtr = 0.028) and for c < 0 it exceeds rtr (at c = −0.02, +0.059 against rtr = 0.019). The population cross-lag correlation departs from a_y q by ≈ 0.94c. At that point the table's rows give sts = 1.2315 and 1.3023 at c = +0.02 and −0.02, and 1.2155 and 1.4111 at c = +0.05 and −0.05, against 1.2588 at c = 0 (`coupling_map_tables.md`). Lagged interaction of |c| = 0.02 therefore moves sts at fixed (r₁, q) by amounts that ∂sts/∂r₁ ≈ 6 at the same point converts to a change in r₁ of the order of a hundredth, and |c| = 0.05 to a few hundredths, and the map's q-axis should not be read as "the contribution of interaction". No value of c is measured on the data; the family's rows are the only scale offered. A second process with the same signature is a pair sharing a slow component, x = s + n_x, y = ±s + n_y, with λ_x, λ_y the shares of var(s) in var(x) and var(y), and a_s and a_n the lag-1 autocorrelations of the shared and the regional parts (no AR(1) form is needed). Then q = ±√(λ_x λ_y), and the true cross-lag correlation exceeds the AR(1) substitution's a_y q by d(x→y) = q (1 − λ_y)(a_s − a_n), with equal loadings q (1 − |q|)(a_s − a_n). That excess is positive when the shared part is slower than the regional parts, and it follows the sign of q. The two are not separable at τ = 1: any positive-definite stationary 4 × 4 matrix of (x_t, y_t, x_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) is the lag-0/lag-1 structure of a VAR(1) with A = Γ₁Γ₀⁻¹, where Γ₁ = E[x_{t+1} x_tᵀ] and Γ₀ is the lag-0 covariance, and innovation covariance Γ₀ − AΓ₀Aᵀ, the Schur complement of the 4 × 4 matrix and so positive definite, which with Γ₀ ≻ 0 makes A stable (ρ(A) < 1) by the discrete Lyapunov theorem; so the sixteen τ = 1 atoms, the residual and CCS-sts of the two processes coincide in population. That is a population statement: a window's sample matrix has unequal diagonal blocks (mean |q_past − q_future| = 0.0168, `residual_source.log`). The worked example λ = 0.25, a_s = 0.95, a_n = 0.80 gives a = 0.8375, q = 0.25, cross-lag correlation 0.2375 and d = +0.028125, exactly the matrix of the symmetric family above with coefficient 0.83, c = 0.03 and q_ε = 0.093 (d = c(1 − q²); `notes/partB12_crosslag_budget.py`, population check).

### The scope map

sts, xtx + yty, ∂sts/∂r₁ and ∂sts/∂q were evaluated on r₁ ∈ [0, 0.95] × q ∈ [−0.6, 0.6] (step 0.01, the grid of Figure 2a and 2b; central differences, h = 0.001), on |q| ≤ 0.95 (the grid of Figure 2c), and on r ∈ [−0.95, 0.95]² for the lag analysis (`notes/partB1_scope_map.md`). Because on this family |∂sts/∂r₁| > |∂sts/∂q| at every grid point except the line r₁ = 0 (Results 2), "r₁-dominated" and "q-dominated" regions are not informative labels; what the map gives is the ratio of the two derivatives at a point. Every pair of subject 1 was placed on it (r₁ = mean of the two regions' within-window lag-1 autocorrelations, q = the pair's within-window lag-0 correlation) for both variants, both runs, windows 1–4 and 6, to read off the operating point and the ratio there.

### The DMT contrast

Per subject the statistic is (post − pre) on DMT minus (post − pre) on PCB (DiD) for the whole-brain mean of a quantity. Pre-injection windows are 1–4 (bins 1–8). The primary post set is windows 6–14 (bins 11–28). Window 5 (bins 9–10) was excluded before any windowed result because both arms of the real-data global fit showed an injection response at bins 8–10 (record, "Pre-registered analysis choices", 13 September 2026; commit febf599). ### The residual diagnostic

For every pair, subject, run and window, the measured 4 × 4 correlation matrix gives the observed sts. The prediction keeps the pair's measured lag-1 autocorrelations a_x, a_y and lag-0 correlation q and replaces the two cross-lag correlations by a_y q and a_x q, the AR(1) structure; predicted sts is the closed-form sts of that matrix, the residual is observed minus predicted, averaged over pairs, and the engine was run on the observed, predicted and residual series. The analysis plan recorded before the run (`notes/partB_prespec_2026-09-14.md`) named two outcomes: a residual DiD near zero, which would show the observed synergy change to be accounted for by autocorrelation change; or a residual DiD both significant and uncorrelated with r₁, to be reported as exploratory evidence of an autocorrelation-independent component. Neither obtained (Results 4). Because the τ = 1 atoms depend only on lag-0 and lag-1 correlations, the diagnostic depends on the AR(1) assumption only through the cross-lag substitution.

**The cross-lag deviation and its budget.** For each pair, d is the measured lag-1 cross-correlation minus the substitution's a_y q (both directions averaged), and δ_run its mean over pairs weighted by s, the sign of the pair's run-level q, so that a positive value is a departure in the direction that lowers sts. Over the run's 14 windows of W = 60 (n_w TRs, SDs σ; π_w ∝ n_w σ_{x,w} σ_{y,w}, ψ_w ∝ n_w σ²_{y,w}, ā_y = Σ_w ψ_w a_{y,w}), δ_run is written as the sum of four terms:

δ_run = δ_within + δ_pool + δ_means + ε,   δ_within = s Σ_w π_w d_w,   δ_pool = s Σ_w π_w (a_{y,w} − ā_y) q_w,   δ_means = δ_run − δ_wd,

with δ_wd the statistic after each region's mean is removed within each window, and ε = δ_wd − δ_within − δ_pool the closing remainder, which collects the effects of window boundaries and standardisation (`notes/rev_crosslag_budget.py`, `partB12_crosslag_budget.py`, `partB13_crosslag_budget_null.py`). The identity is therefore exact by construction, δ_within and δ_pool being the two terms built independently; |ε| is 0.00002 on `ts_gsr` and 0.00005 on `ts_demean` (grand means; `notes/review_results/partB/crosslag_budget_tables.md`). For the budget the finite-sample null described below was simulated as 840-TR runs per run type, with its mean filter parameter β̄ and a two-component mixture for q solved to the data's run-level mean a, mean |q̂| and fraction |q̂| < 0.05. Six configurations were run (heterogeneity 0.5, 0.25, 1.0; placebo ACF for both runs; Gaussian q; window-level targets), ≥ 25,000 pair-runs each. Two controls, a shared slow component (λ = 0.2, a_s − a_n = 0.10) and pooling (q and filter changing at TR 240), were scored the same way. Null-corrected terms are data minus null; the reading rules were recorded before the run (record, "The cross-lag budget: pre-run entry", 16 September 2026, 16:24 UTC).

Two additions were made in the revision after the second review, with rules recorded before they were run (record, "Part B, items 6–8", pre-run entry of 15 September, 07:30 UTC): the CCS definition check (above) and a split-half test of the per-subject correlation between the residual DiD and the CCS-sts DiD (`notes/partB7_splithalf.py`). In the split-half test both DiDs were recomputed on odd windows only (pre 1, 3; post 7, 9, 11, 13) and on even windows only (pre 2, 4; post 6, 8, 10, 12, 14), and the within-half correlations were compared with the cross-half ones, which shared window noise cannot produce. The rule, fixed in advance on `ts_gsr`, was: not window noise if both cross-half correlations are positive and their mean is at least half the within-half mean. In that case the two are reported together as one exploratory observation of an autocorrelation-independent component whose sign matches the original pre-specified direction, with the statement that it arose after four specification changes and requires an independent dataset. Estimation noise if the mean cross-half correlation is below half the within-half mean or either is negative; in that case both are reported as null with the test as the evidence. That rule was set without reference to the split-half reliabilities of the two quantities, and at the reliabilities observed its pass criterion lay above the largest cross-half correlation two perfectly correlated reliable components could produce, so the test could not discriminate. The flaw is recorded as such (record, correction note of 15 September) and the outcome is reported as undetermined (Results 4). A third computation, the finite-sample null of the residual (`notes/review_v2_residual_null.py`), is not an addition of ours: it was written and run by the second adversarial review, before any record entry and with no recorded rule (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix B), and is reported here as a review computation. In it each pair is one linear filter applied to two white noises with correlation q, so the population cross-lag correlation is exactly r₁q and every residual is finite-sample. The filter (band-pass 0.01–0.08 Hz times exp(−βf²)) is fitted to the pooled placebo autocorrelation function (lags 1–6 fitted 0.872, 0.549, 0.179, −0.091, −0.196, −0.168). For the DMT-post cell it is fitted to the post-injection DMT function. β and q vary across pairs so that the window-level mean pair a and |q| equal the four run × period cells of `residual_source.log` (DMT and placebo, pre-injection and primary windows); 3,000 pairs × 50 windows per cell, a cell being here one of those run × period combinations. The null is stationary by construction. The third review re-ran it (it reproduces) and varied two of its free choices, the per-pair heterogeneity of the filter and whether the DMT-post autocorrelation shape is applied; the paper quotes the range those variations give (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_second_pass_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix, computation 3). One technicality of the residual's inference: its phase-randomised null randomises the observed TR-resolution series against the fixed per-window prediction, so it is a null of the observed series' temporal structure rather than of the residual's construction; the sign-flip test and the bootstrap carry the residual inference.

### Bias simulations, the matched null, lag variants and deconvolution

Finite-sample bias of the windowed estimator was characterised before any windowed result on simulated VAR(1) processes with analytic ground truth (`02_bias_check.py`, 2,000 replicate windows per cell). The estimated between-condition sts difference had the true sign in every cell, with 52–77 % of the true difference absorbed at W = 30 and 15–45 % at W = 60, which is why W = 60 became the primary window with W = 30 as a positive control (record, "Pre-registered analysis choices"). The real regional autocorrelation function decays faster than either simulated family, so no simulated shrinkage figure is applied to the real contrast. The sts-matched null (a review computation; `notes/rev_sts_matched_null.py`) asked whether a covariance change at equal analytic sts can manufacture a difference. It used three families (the record's asymmetric VAR(1); a VAR(1) at a = 0.87; Gaussian processes with the data's own placebo and post-DMT autocorrelation functions), which yield four matched pairs. In the first family these are a lowered autocorrelation with the cross-coupling re-solved (F1-i) and a raised noise correlation with the autocorrelation re-solved (F1-ii). In the second only the noise-correlation shift can be matched (F2-ii), since no cross-coupling or noise correlation restores the analytic sts after a 0.02 drop in a. In the third the DMT-post spectrum was tilted to restore the placebo r₁ (F3-b), matching by q alone yielding no pair. The second condition's parameters were solved so that the analytic sts is unchanged, and the difference was estimated over 4,000 independent windows at W = 30, 60 and 840, as mean(shifted) − mean(baseline) with its standard error and as a share of the real primary DiD, −0.0809 (review, section 5). The decomposition was repeated with the lag-τ four-vector (x_t, y_t, x_{t+τ}, y_{t+τ}) for τ = 2, 3, 5 on `ts_gsr`, windowed and global, with the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast built in the same way; the analysis plan recorded beforehand was that the lag-τ autocorrelation r_τ falls with τ and the artefact weakens. As a post-hoc exploration the regional series were deconvolved with rsHRF 1.7.0 (Wu et al., 2013, 2021) and the pipeline rerun unchanged (review, section 3; S2 Text).

### 2. The scope map, the operating point of real pairs, and what DMT changes

On the AR(1) family sts is even in q, decreasing in |q| and steeply increasing in r₁ (Figure 2a): at q = 0 it is 0.041 (r₁ = 0.2), 0.446 (0.6), 1.022 (0.8), 1.282 (0.85), 1.661 (0.9), 2.328 (0.95). At r₁ = 0.85 it falls from 1.282 to 1.131 as |q| goes from 0 to 0.6 (`notes/review_results/partB/scope_map_tables.md`). |∂sts/∂r₁| exceeds |∂sts/∂q| at every grid point except the line r₁ = 0: on the requested grid, on |q| ≤ 0.95 (0 of 18,336 grid points) and on r ∈ [−0.95, 0.95]² (0 of 36,481). The family therefore has no region in which q contributes comparably, and the fact that all of a subject's pairs fall in the r₁-dominated region carries no information about the pairs. What the overlay establishes is where the pairs sit and how large the ratio is there (Figure 2c, which draws one of the overlay's 20 variant × run × window combinations (Methods) — `ts_gsr`, the DMT run, window 6, the window of the per-pair checks below — with a seeded random subsample of 1,500 of its 6,555 pairs; `scripts/15_figures_v2.py`). At the operating point (0.85, 0.25), used for all derivative values quoted in this paper, the derivatives are 6.07 and −0.19 nats per unit (ratio 32); at (0.85, 0.6) they are 5.71 and −0.59 (10). ### 4. The residual diagnostic, its finite-sample null, and the shared variance with CCS-sts

Table 4 and Figure 4 summarise the diagnostic (`notes/review_results/partB/diag_tables.md`, `notes/review_results/inference_rows_diag.csv`). The prediction from each pair's measured (a_x, a_y, q) alone reproduces the observed sts level to within 4.3 % (the mean over all subjects, both runs and all windows, 1.1377 at W = 60) and the whole-run level to 1.1 % (1.2883, the mean over subjects and runs of the run-level fit), over-predicting in both. Across the 392 subject × run × window combinations the residual SD is a fifth of the observed sts's. A symmetric-coefficient variant of the prediction (one coefficient for both regions; not in the analysis plan, reported as a comparison) over-predicts by 16 %. The predicted DiD has the observed sign in every cell, over-shoots it by 14–29 %, and tracks it per subject at r = 0.98–0.99.

The residual DiD is positive in every cell, with a CI excluding zero and a sign-flip p below 0.05 in all four windowed cells (Table 4: +0.0115 [+0.0021, +0.0211], p = 0.042 at the primary cell; the phase-randomised p is also below 0.05 in each, with the technicality of Methods). It survives FD residualisation and is concentrated in the drug-present windows (early set +0.0179 [+0.0099, +0.0260], p = 0.0012, phase p = 0.003, positive in 12 of 14; late set +0.0064, p = 0.32). Within each run alone the change is not significant: DMT post − pre +0.0077 [−0.0001, +0.0149], p = 0.080; placebo −0.0038 [−0.0091, +0.0014], p = 0.19; the placebo run contributes 0.33 of the DiD (`notes/review_results/inference_rows_diag.csv`), so the DiD's significance rests on the two runs moving in opposite directions, a third of it on the placebo run. Neither outcome named in the analysis plan obtained. The residual DiD is not near zero: the plan's "near zero" branch is read as a residual DiD whose subject-bootstrap 95 % CI includes zero (a reading stated in the record's entry for the revision after the plain-language companion, 15 September 2026, 15:46 UTC, and not in the plan), and the observed CI, [+0.0021, +0.0211], does not include zero. Nor is it uncorrelated with r₁: the subjects whose r₁ fell more show the larger over-prediction (r = −0.780), so the plan's signature of an autocorrelation-independent component is not met. The residual is a pre-specified test whose outcome fell outside both recorded branches; it is not relabelled by that outcome. A post-hoc supplement (`ts_gsr` W = 60; `notes/review_results/partB/residual_source.log`) traced the level residual to the diagnostic's single model assumption, corr(x_t, y_{t+1}) = a_y q. The cross-lag substitution alone reproduces it (−0.0489 of −0.0489). The measured cross-lag correlations scatter about a_y q with a signed mean offset of +0.00014 (a signed mean, in which pairs of opposite q cancel; see below), and the residual is more negative where pair a and |q| are higher (R² = 0.74 on pair a, pair |q| and the cross-lag scatter). Those correlations cannot decide whether the positive residual DiD is that over-prediction shrinking as a and |q| fall under DMT or a component that scales with the drug effect, because both predict them. The finite-sample null (Methods; a computation of the second review, with no pre-recorded rule) can, within the limits of its construction. With the data's own autocorrelation function, pair heterogeneity and the four cells' operating points, it gives a residual level of −0.035 (−3.0 %) against the observed −0.049 (−4.3 %) and a residual DiD of +0.0054 against the observed +0.0115, reproducing the DMT-run change (+0.0041 against +0.0077) and not the placebo-run change (−0.0013 against −0.0038). It gives a level of −8.5 %, −3.1 % and −0.34 % at W = 30, 60 and 840 against the data's −9.7 %, −4.3 % and −1.1 % (`notes/review_results/logs/review_v2_residual_null.log`). That value depends on choices the null leaves free: in the third review's re-run, per-pair filter heterogeneity of 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 of the mean gives a null DiD of +0.0059, +0.0052 and +0.0077, and applying the placebo autocorrelation shape to every cell gives +0.0037 (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_second_pass_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix, computation 3). So the null accounts for a third to two-thirds of the observed residual DiD, and the observed interval contains every one of those values. The direction of the residual DiD and a third to two-thirds of its size are therefore what the cross-lag assumption produces under finite sampling. The remainder of the residual DiD is not established: its excess over the null is not significant at any of the null's values (exact sign-flip p = 0.15 at +0.0037, 0.25 at +0.0054 and 0.47 at +0.0077, each value treated as fixed). What does exceed the null is the run-level residual on `ts_gsr`, −0.0137 [−0.0145, −0.0130], negative in 14 of 14 subjects, against the null's −0.0045 (−1.1 % against −0.34 %; sign-flip p = 0.0001; like the p values above, a computation of the fresh review, `notes/fresh_review_2026-09-17/checks/check_C1_residual_vs_null.log`, with no pre-recorded rule); on `ts_demean` the run-level residual is +0.1 %, i.e. absent (Table 4). That null is stationary, and the runs are not, by design: the DMT run carries an injection at TR 240 and the placebo run an r₁ and variance drift across the session (window variance / run variance 1.28 → 0.89 on DMT, 0.94 → 1.06 on placebo; review, section 6).

The diagnostic's one assumption, corr(x_t, y_{t+1}) = a_y q, was tested on the cross-lag correlations themselves. Their departure from it, d, lowers sts where q > 0 and raises it where q < 0 (reversing one region's sign reverses both and changes no atom). A mechanism acting through the cross-lag correlations therefore shows in δ_run, the mean of d over pairs weighted by the sign of the pair's run-level q, so that a positive value is a departure in the direction that lowers sts; a signed mean cancels on `ts_gsr`, where 54.5 % of pairs have q < 0. At the run level δ_run = +0.00340 [+0.00300, +0.00380], 14 of 14 subjects (DMT run +0.00381, placebo +0.00299): on the family's scale 44 % of the −0.0137 run-level residual at the operating point, 37 % at the pairs' run-level mean point (a 0.8666, |q| 0.1945), a scale rather than a decomposition. Four accounts leave that signature: finite sampling; pooling of windows that differ in a, q or variance under one fit; lagged coupling; and a slow component shared by the two regions, d = q(1 − λ_y)(a_s − a_n) (Methods). The last two are one account at τ = 1, every stationary lag-0/lag-1 structure being that of a VAR(1) with A = Γ₁Γ₀⁻¹; the worked example (λ = 0.25, a_s = 0.95, a_n = 0.80) is the coupled family at coefficient 0.83, c = 0.03, q_ε = 0.093 (Methods). The exact budget δ_run = δ_within + δ_pool + δ_means + ε (Methods) was read under rules recorded before the run, against a finite-sample null solved to the data (six configurations) and two controls. Finite sampling is a minor part: the null's δ_run is +0.00029 to +0.00046, 8–14 % of the observed δ_run (the null's value depends on the density of q̂ near zero, to which each configuration is solved; Methods). Of the null-corrected +0.00301 [+0.00261, +0.00340], the within-window term is most (+0.00250, 83–84 % in every configuration; a shared-slow-component control puts 90 % there, a pooling control 30 %, and in that control the within-window term equals the null's (+0.00040), i.e. it is finite sampling), pooling a minor part (+0.00046, 15 %) and the window means a minor part (+0.00004). Pooling is larger on the DMT run (DMT − placebo +0.00039 per subject, sign-flip p = 0.0006, 12 of 14; 11 % of that run's +0.00349). So the departure is present inside 2-minute windows, where pooling across windows cannot act; pooling is not its principal account, and the DMT run carries about a tenth more of it. On `ts_demean` δ_run is −0.00262 [−0.00495, −0.00037], reported without interpretation (Discussion). What acts inside the windows — common slow drive or lagged coupling, non-stationarity faster than two minutes, or a within-window finite-sample effect the null as specified does not reproduce — and what produces the rest of the run-level residual, is not located (the residual DiD's remainder is not established, above; S9 Table; record, "The cross-lag budget: outcome", 16 September 2026).

The residual's per-subject variance is shared with CCS-sts. Across subjects the residual DiD (W = 60) correlates with the CCS-sts DiD at r = +0.799 on `ts_gsr` at W = 60, +0.094 at the `ts_gsr` global fit, +0.875 on `ts_demean` at W = 60 and +0.676 at the `ts_demean` global fit (`ccs_pub_tables.md`). Figure 3b shows the W = 60 pair on `ts_gsr`. The rule's pass criterion of 0.41 lay above that ceiling: its "not window noise" branch was unreachable at these reliabilities, the rule was set without reference to the ceiling, and that is a flaw in the rule, recorded as such (record, correction note of 15 September). What the numbers say is this. The observed cross-half correlation of 0.32 is 0.82 of the ceiling (its disattenuated value), which is consistent with a shared reliable component; the reliabilities are too low to distinguish that from a partial one, or, at N = 14 (cross-half p = 0.59 and 0.08), from none. The verdict is undetermined. The test does establish that most of the within-half correlation (0.815 against a ceiling of 0.39) is estimation noise common to the same windows and that the reliable part of each quantity is small. The group-mean DiDs of both quantities are positive in both halves (residual +0.0067 / +0.0164; CCS-sts +0.0033 / +0.0054). No autocorrelation-independent component is reported: the residual and the CCS-sts change remain exploratory, they arose after four specification changes (windowed sts → sixteen atoms → CCS → diagnostic residual), and a pre-specified test on an independent dataset is what would settle whether their shared component is signal. The relation between the residual and the windowed estimator's manufacture (Results 6) is the same question from the other side: both are the part of the windowed DiD that the family does not account for. The run-level residual (`ts_gsr`, −1.1 % against the null's −0.34 %) is the estimate least exposed to manufacture and the one the non-stationarity of the runs bears on most.

### 5. Lag dependence

Table 6 and Figure 5 give the lag variants (`ts_gsr`, W = 60; `notes/review_results/partB/lag_tables.md`). As the plan anticipated, r_τ falls with τ and the artefact weakens: the sts contrast is significant on both nulls at both estimators wherever the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast is significant by sign-flip (τ = 1, 2, 3; the sts contrast's phase-randomised p = 0.0020, 0.0010 and 0.0010 at W = 60 and 0.0010 at the global fit, Table 6; the autocorrelation contrast's is 0.0729, 0.0040 and 0.0020, so at τ = 1 it misses that null, Results 2) and null where it is not (τ = 5; W = 60 p = 0.3688, phase p = 0.2607; global fit +0.0015, p = 0.7997, phase p = 0.7692). Per subject it tracks the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast, with the sign reversed at τ = 5 because r₅ is negative and every AR(1) atom is even in the autocorrelation. At τ = 3 the tracking is only partial: the per-subject correlation is 0.626 at W = 60 (0.885 at the global fit) while the sts contrast is significant on both nulls and survives FD residualisation. So at τ = 3 the sts contrast is only partly the r₃ contrast, which is either a limit of the map at low r_τ (r₃ = 0.14, where ∂sts/∂r is small and the estimator's scatter is a larger share of the atom) or a second remainder; the data do not say which. The lag-τ autocorrelation contrast itself grows with τ (−0.0146, −0.0468, −0.0681 at τ = 1, 2, 3; Table 6). Relative to the sts level at each lag the sts contrast is −7 %, −24 % and −16 % at τ = 1, 2, 3 (−0.0809 of 1.1554, −0.0429 of 0.1797, −0.0043 of 0.0262; Table 6), so in relative terms it does not weaken; and the bootstrap intervals of the three autocorrelation contrasts overlap ([−0.0251, −0.0052], [−0.0754, −0.0174], [−0.1120, −0.0213]; `inference_rows_lag.csv`), so their growth with τ is a statement about point estimates. DMT changes the shape of the regional autocorrelation function towards a faster decay, of which the r₁ change is the lag-1 summary, and the in-band spectral centroid — the power-weighted mean frequency within 0.01–0.08 Hz, a review computation (`rev_extra.py`) — rises accordingly (+0.0023 Hz [+0.0006, +0.0040], p = 0.022, 12 of 14 on `ts_gsr`; review, section 1). The paper does not interpret the shape change beyond this: no pre-specified quantity addresses it, and the τ = 1 estimator the literature uses sees only its lag-1 summary. It is stated so that "DMT lowers r₁" is not taken for the whole of the effect on the autocorrelation function, and it is one candidate for why the τ = 3 sts contrast tracks r₃ only partly. No lag moves the pairs to a point where q contributes comparably, because no such point exists on the family at BOLD cross-correlations.

### 6. Estimator manufacture, and the global fit for between-state contrasts

Where an sts-matched pair of conditions can be built (equal analytic sts, different covariance), the windowed estimator manufactures a difference at W = 60 of −9 % (F1-i), +16 % (F1-ii), +7 % ± 8 % (F2-ii) and −8 % ± 6 % (F3-b) of the real primary DiD of −0.0809, of either sign; the ± figures are the standard errors over the 4,000 windows of the two pairs whose difference is not distinguishable from zero (review, section 5). At W = 30 it manufactures up to +33 %. At the full run length it is ≤ 2 % except in one matched pair (F2-ii, +29 %) (review, section 5; a review computation). A matched pair in which r₁ falls by the observed amount could not be built at data-like autocorrelation, because the analytic sts is a steep function of r₁ (≈ +0.07 nats per +0.01 near 0.87) and a weak function of q. The observed autocorrelation-function change alone, at fixed q, changes the true sts by −0.069 against the observed DiD of −0.081, and the W = 60 estimator returns −0.056 of it. This is the third figure the paper gives for the sts change that the autocorrelation change produces, beside the map projection of Results 2 (−0.087) and the per-pair diagnostic's prediction of Results 4 (−0.0924, the 114 % of the Abstract). The three answer three questions: the mean r₁ change passed through the slope at one point; each pair's own (a_x, a_y, q) averaged over pairs; and the change in the true sts under the whole autocorrelation-function change, before the estimator's finite-sample shrinkage. All three have the observed sign, and the differences between them are the residual and the manufacture figures seen from different sides: the diagnostic's over-shoot of the observed DiD is the residual DiD of Results 4, and the gap between the true change (−0.069) and what the estimator returns of it (−0.056) is the shrinkage characterised above. The windowed whole-brain sts sits 0.1531 nats below the global fit in the DMT pre-injection windows (1.1554 against 1.3085); the diagnostic decomposes that gap into the lower within-window parameters, which the prediction from the window's own (a_x, a_y, q) captures, and a residual of −0.0489 against −0.0137 at the run level. On manufacture grounds the global fit is the better estimator for a contrast between separately fitted runs or groups: at 840 samples it manufactures ≤ 2 % in three of the four matched pairs (+29 % in the fourth) and its run-level residual is 1.1 % on `ts_gsr` (0.1 % on `ts_demean`). Two things qualify that recommendation, and it rests on the manufacture figures alone. First, the global fit's per-bin values are local atoms under a single run-level covariance, not per-bin decompositions. A pre/post contrast within a run — the contrast this paper runs — is therefore exposed to the within-run variance change that Results 3 gives as a reason for not reading the CCS global-fit cells. On the DMT run the ratio of window variance to run variance goes from 1.28 to 0.89, and the global-fit sts bins correlate with that ratio at +0.37 per run on average (review, section 1). The windowed series follow the same ratio more closely — windowed sts at +0.49 and window-standardised r₁ at +0.54 per run on average, +0.77 and +0.73 on the group-mean series (review, section 6) — but not by that route: each window is standardised and fitted on its own samples, so its variance level cannot enter its atoms, and the correlation records that in these runs variance falls in the windows where r₁ falls. Variance can move the windowed contrast only through the window's correlation matrix, which the diagnostic models; partialling the ratio out of the relation across the 28 group-mean windows leaves r = +0.95 (from +0.97 on the series as printed to three decimals, 0.977 unrounded, Results 3; a computation of the fresh review, `notes/fresh_review_2026-09-17/checks/check_C2_variance_ratio.log`). No contrast is residualised on the ratio: for the windowed estimator that would remove the part of the r₁ change that coincides with the variance change, not an artefact of scale. The global-fit MMI-sts contrast (−0.0801 against −0.0809 at W = 60) is therefore quoted as agreement between two estimators, not as an independent confirmation. Second, the global fit on `ts_gsr` is the one cell of four in which sts fell less than proportionally to TDMI (Results 3, +0.0204, p = 0.025), so the estimator recommended here is the one at which the proportionality the map predicts does not hold; the recommendation is not made on that ground. The windowed W = 60 estimator remains this paper's primary because it was the pre-specified one and the two agree.

## Discussion

### What the finding is and is not

The analysis says nothing about whether synergy in the intended sense is present in the brain; what it establishes is what the estimator responds to. On the bivariate AR(1) family the Gaussian-MMI synergy atom is self-prediction plus rtr, balanced by four negative atoms, of the kind the ΦID authors note MMI can produce (Mediano et al., 2025, SI Appendix; Luppi et al., 2026), that have no counterpart under CCS. Its dependence on r₁ dominates its dependence on q at every point of the map at which BOLD pairs sit. This is a property of the estimator on a family without lagged interaction; on the coupled family lagged interaction moves sts by comparable amounts at fixed (r₁, q). What follows for a between-state contrast is narrow. When the states differ in r₁ and not comparably in lagged interaction, the MMI-sts contrast is dominated by that difference, scaled by ∂sts/∂r₁, whether or not anything the word "synergy" is meant to capture has changed. On this dataset the cross-lag departure through which lagged interaction shows at τ = 1 is measured (Results 4) and differs little between the runs (sign(q)-weighted +0.0038 on the DMT run against +0.0030 on placebo, whole runs; about 0.0015 nats on the family's scale at the operating point). Here the per-pair diagnostic's prediction from each pair's (a_x, a_y, q) gives the sign and 114 % of the magnitude of the sts change (Results 4), and the per-subject collinearity is 0.95 (which includes estimation error the two DiDs share; Results 3). What the dataset shows is that, not "sts change is autocorrelation change". The pre-specified criterion under which it would have shown more — a residual DiD near zero — was not met (Results 4).

Three things the data add are stated as they are. First, the identity's excess has the wrong sign on real BOLD: sts lies below xtx + yty by 0.084 nats where the family puts it above by rtr. The closed form is therefore an account of the estimator's structure and not an exact model of the data. Second, the residual the diagnostic leaves is positive in every cell (p = 0.042 at the primary cell, which the weighting rule of Methods does not treat as established), larger in the early post-injection windows, and in the direction of the original hypothesis. A finite-sample null, a review computation, accounts for a third to two-thirds of it depending on the null's free choices. The rest of the residual DiD is not distinguishable from the null, every null value lying inside its interval; the run-level residual on `ts_gsr` is, at three times the null's (−0.0137 [−0.0145, −0.0130] against −0.0045; absent, +0.1 %, on `ts_demean`), and it is not accounted for by the null as specified. That null is a stationary construction, and the runs are non-stationary by design. Third, the cross-lag correlations depart from the AR(1) substitution in the direction that lowers sts, in every subject (sign(q)-weighted run-level deviation +0.0034). On the family's scale that is 44 % of the run-level residual at the operating point and 37 % at the pairs' run-level mean point (a 0.8666, |q| 0.1945). An exact budget of the departure against a finite-sample null solved to the data puts most of it inside 2-minute windows (83–84 %). Finite sampling and pooling are a minor part each (8–14 % and 15 %), with pooling larger on the DMT run by a tenth of that run's signature (Results 4). What acts inside the windows is not identified. Common slow drive and lagged coupling are one account at τ = 1, since every stationary lag-0/lag-1 structure is that of a VAR(1). Neither is separated from non-stationarity faster than two minutes or from a within-window finite-sample effect absent from the null as specified. On `ts_demean` the run-level statistic is negative (−0.0026), which a shared part slower than the regional parts would not produce, and it is reported without interpretation.

The residual's per-subject variance is largely shared with CCS-sts. The split-half test meant to tell shared signal from shared window noise could not, because its pre-recorded threshold lay above the reliability ceiling of the two quantities; that question is undetermined. The cross-half correlation is 0.82 of the ceiling (its disattenuated value), which is consistent with a shared reliable component. The reliabilities (0.49, 0.30) cannot distinguish that from a partial one or, at N = 14, from none.

What is claimed is the account of the estimator on the AR(1) family and, on this dataset, the collinearity of the sts and r₁ contrasts and the diagnostic's reproduction of the contrast's sign and 114 % of its size. No autocorrelation-independent synergy change is reported from these data. The residual and the CCS-sts change stay exploratory, having arisen after four specification changes. An independent dataset with a pre-specified test of the residual and of CCS-sts is what would settle it.

### Redundancy functions, null models, and what CCS costs

The property was examined under two redundancy functions and is not found in CCS-sts under the published double-redundancy definition to the degree MMI-sts shows it; I_min, I_BROJA, the dependency-based and the information-geometric Gaussian decompositions were not examined and any of them could share or not share it, so "MMI-specific" is not a licensed phrase. What CCS costs is what the construction makes explicit. Its double redundancy is a masked double co-information, so CCS-sts is the average over all samples of a quantity that is zero where the five local signs agree and −c where they disagree (Methods); it takes negative values, and its whole-brain value depends on which fifth sign the mask uses (−0.048 under the published definition against −0.039 under `phyid`'s, with the qualitative statements unchanged). Its DMT increase is consistent across subjects at the global fit and survives the motion and trend controls there and at the primary cell, and it is not read, for the reasons given in Results 3. Null-model normalisation of atoms (Liardi et al., 2025: each atom read as a quantile against an ensemble of systems with the same total mutual information, developed for PID on MEG and named there as a future extension to ΦID) is the ΦID authors' remedy for the dependence of atoms on total mutual information; on the family that dependence is r₁'s route into sts, since TDMI = 2S depends on r₁ alone. The diagnostic here is different in kind — per pair, analytic, and specific to the lag-1 autocorrelation — and the two are complementary: a null that preserves total mutual information does not preserve autocorrelation, and the diagnostic does not address the lattice's negativity. CCS is not offered as the corrected estimator, only as the one whose sts does not carry the autocorrelation contrast the way MMI-sts does.

### A distinction for applicability

Two uses of MMI-sts should be distinguished. A contrast between states, between groups, or across a session is exposed wherever the compared conditions differ in r₁: BOLD lag-1 autocorrelation rises in propofol sedation and falls in deep anaesthesia and in disorders of consciousness (Huang et al., 2018), under DMT as this dataset shows, and, within a placebo run, with time (the placebo run's r₁ drift carries 0.34 of the DiD here). Between-group designs are exposed to any group difference in r₁ from physiology, motion, age or scanner. A spatial map of synergy across regions is exposed through regional differences in r₁: on the map higher r₁ gives higher sts with rtr almost unmoved, so a regional autocorrelation gradient would produce a synergy-minus-redundancy gradient of the same spatial form, and within a single window half of the between-pair variance of sts here is pair r₁, while across regions three-quarters of the between-region variance of the placebo-baseline sts map is regional r₁ (r = +0.863; sts − rtr, +0.792; Results 2), so the exposure is real on this dataset. The applicability table (S3 Text), which lists nine empirical studies (Down et al., 2026; Gatica et al., 2024; Luppi et al., 2022, 2023, 2024, 2026; Nago et al., 2026; Tarchi et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025), gives every study — all nine now read in full; Tarchi et al. (2026) does not state its redundancy function — the same conditional statement — the sign of r₁ change that would produce its reported effect on the map — and that statement is what the map predicts, not evidence about any study, since none of them reports regional r₁. We apply it to all of them: every study in the table receives the same sentence, and none is singled out. Luppi et al. (2022) falls outside the TR range the plan fixed for the table, a scoping rule and not a judgement. By the map it would be the most exposed dataset in the table: at its stated TR of 0.72 s and 0.008–0.09 Hz band (Luppi et al., 2022, Methods) the ideal-band-pass r₁ is ≈ 0.97, where ∂sts/∂r₁ = 32.8 against ∂rtr/∂r₁ = 0.06 (`family_checks.log`); its measured regional r₁ is not reported. Its primary analysis is HRF-deconvolved (Wu et al., 2013), the gradient is replicated without deconvolution (its Extended Data Fig. 2c), and the authors report that deconvolution 'had negligible effects on synergy and redundancy calculations' at that TR; our own evidence that deconvolution leaves the mechanism in place (S2 Text) comes from TR 2 s data and does not transfer to TR 0.72 s. That study also contains the one test of the exposure in the table: its authors recomputed synergy and redundancy on phase-randomised surrogates that keep each region's autocorrelation and remove the correlations between regions (Luppi et al., 2022, Methods; their Table 1). On the family that surrogate sits on the q = 0 line, where MMI sts of a pair is twice the self-information of its less autocorrelated member and rtr is zero, so a region's surrogate synergy strength is a monotone function of its r₁ and the surrogate gradient is, up to noise, the regional r₁ gradient. Its correlations with the macroscale maps were small and, with one exception (gyrification index, ρ = 0.26, spin p = 0.028), not significant, against 0.40–0.54 for the observed gradient. On that dataset, therefore, the autocorrelation-only gradient does not reproduce the observed gradient's macroscale associations; that is evidence against the exposure for those associations, and it is the only such evidence in the table. It leaves open what this paper measures on its own data, the correlation of the observed map with regional r₁ (+0.792 for sts − rtr here), which that study does not report. Two limits on reach follow from the identities rtr + sts = TDMI (so ∂rtr/∂q = −∂sts/∂q) and ∂rtr/∂r₁ ≈ 0. First, a change in q moves synergy and redundancy oppositely by equal amounts while a change in r₁ leaves rtr almost untouched, so on the family a pattern of synergy and redundancy moving in opposite directions (Down et al., 2026) is the q signature or a mixture and not the r₁ signature. That is a statement about the family, which the data follow only loosely (on `ts_gsr` MMI rtr fell by a tenth of the sts fall, −0.0078 against −0.0809, Table 1, where the family gives a hundredth), and it is the one place the table argues against the mechanism. Second, ΦR = rtr = −½ ln(1 − r₁²q²) on the symmetric family, which falls with a fall in either factor, so the r₁-dominance result for sts does not carry over to ΦR (Luppi et al., 2024, 2026). DMT produced the r₁ fall on both variants and the |q| fall on `ts_gsr` only, the signed mean correlation having risen on `ts_demean` (Results 2). Among the studies in the table, Luppi et al. (2022) tested an autocorrelation-preserving null as described above; none of the others addressed autocorrelation, and Varley (2024) is the study whose subject it is.

### Recommendations

Report the mean lag-1 autocorrelation beside sts, per condition and subject, and its contrast beside the sts contrast; if the two are collinear across subjects, test the remainder with the diagnostic rather than discarding it, since here a 0.95 collinearity left a remainder with a sign-flip p of 0.042, reported and not treated as established (Methods, Multiplicity). Run the residual diagnostic: predict each pair's sts from its measured (a_x, a_y, q), test the residual with the same inference as the observed contrast, compare it with a finite-sample null whose free choices are varied and reported, and state the cross-lag assumption. For a contrast between separately fitted runs or groups, prefer the global fit on manufacture grounds (≤ 2 % in three of the four matched pairs at 840 samples; run-level residual 1 % on `ts_gsr`, 0.1 % on `ts_demean`). A pre/post contrast within one run under a single fit is exposed to within-run variance change and is not what the global fit is recommended for (Results 3, 6). Consider CCS under the published definition, with the costs above. Consider τ > 1, with its signal cost: a longer lag reduces the artefact by reducing everything, and at no lag do the pairs reach a point where q contributes comparably (Results 5). HRF deconvolution, tried post hoc here, left the sts contrast and its collinearity with the autocorrelation contrast in place and changed ΦR's baseline and drift behaviour in ways the raw series only partly share (S2 Text); it is not a remedy on these data.

### Limitations

One dataset, one drug, one parcellation, N = 14; effect sizes are reported with CIs and the DMT contrast is a proof of concept. The AR(1) family does not fit the data beyond lag 1 and its excess identity has the wrong sign on them; the map's q-axis and the diagnostic's cross-lag substitution are AR(1) statements, the coupled family is symmetric, and of the residual DiD they leave a third to two-thirds is finite-sample under one stationary null and the rest is not distinguishable from it, while the run-level residual on `ts_gsr` exceeds that null. There is no ground truth for synergy in these data: the analysis establishes what the estimator responds to, not what the brain does. Two redundancy functions were examined, both Gaussian and continuous; the discrete CCS on binarised data used elsewhere was not; the CCS definition used is the published one (Mediano et al., 2025, SI Appendix, Definition 2, identical to the arXiv text). The windowed estimator's finite-sample manufacture is not removed by the diagnostic, and the split-half test's pre-recorded rule was set without reference to the reliability ceiling of the quantities it compares, above which its threshold turned out to lie, so that test decided nothing. An odd/even-TR split within windows was not used because adjacent TRs share most of their variance at r₁ ≈ 0.86, so the two halves would not be independent; odd/even windows is the right design, and it is underpowered here (about 20 effective samples per window, N = 14). Two computations earlier drafts listed as not run were run in the revision after the plain-language companion with predictions recorded first: the regional test of the spatial-map claim, whose predicted branch obtained (Results 2), and the run-level cross-lag deviation, whose statistic and reading were corrected twice afterwards (History; the superseded values in S9 Table). Its current reading rests on the budget of Results 4 under rules recorded before the run: finite sampling a minor part, the within-window term most, pooling a minor part and larger on the DMT run; the budget does not say what acts inside the windows, and common slow drive and lagged coupling are one account at τ = 1. Its null is the finite-sample null as specified, with a symmetric q distribution where 54.5 % of the data's pairs have q < 0, and the statistic on `ts_demean` (−0.0026 at the run level) is reported and not read. Many statistics are reported without correction (Methods). The pre-specification history is as stated in Methods: the confirmatory test was of a different hypothesis, its directional-failure rule was written after the 115-region decrease was known, and the analysis plans of Part B were written after the review computations had characterised the phenomenon. Of the additions made in the revision after the second review, two (the CCS definition check and the split-half test) had rules recorded on the day they were run, the coupled family was entered with no prediction, and the finite-sample null is a review computation with no recorded rule. The weighting rule by which results are treated as established or not (Methods, Multiplicity) was written after every result it governs existed and is not pre-specified. The git record establishes ordering, not blindness.
