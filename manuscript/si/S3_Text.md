## 2. The CCS definition check

Under MMI each single-target redundancy is the smaller of the two single-source mutual informations (Barrett, 2015) and the double redundancy rtr is the smallest of the four single-source, single-target mutual informations (Mediano et al., 2021, Appendix, Definition 2). Under CCS (Ince, 2017) a single-target redundancy is the local co-information at the samples where the constituent local mutual informations and the co-information share a sign, and zero elsewhere. The ΦID double redundancy under CCS is defined by Mediano et al. (2021, Appendix, Definition 1; 2025, SI Appendix, Definition 2) as the pointwise double co-information c(x; y), summed over the samples at which all marginal pointwise mutual informations and the pointwise full mutual information i(x; y) share a sign. The double co-information is the signed sum, an inclusion–exclusion over the lattice, of the local mutual informations at the fifteen nodes of the product lattice other than its bottom node rtr (the sixteen atom positions, formed as the product of the past and future redundancy lattices). `phyid` implements a different mask: it keeps c at the samples where the four single-source, single-target local mutual informations and c itself share a sign, so its fifth sign condition is that of the double co-information rather than of the full mutual information. On the lattice c ≡ rtr − sts (deviation ≤ 3 × 10⁻¹⁵; the SI Appendix of Mediano et al., 2025, states the same identity, c(x; y) = I∂^{{1}{2}→{1}{2}} − I∂^{{12}→{12}}), so under either mask CCS-sts is 0 at the selected samples and −c elsewhere, and its whole-brain value is the average of that quantity over all samples. An earlier check (`notes/partB2_ccs.md`) had verified `phyid`'s single-target CCS against an independent implementation of Ince's construction (agreement 2.1 × 10⁻¹³ over 200 random draws of the real data; 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ on a 56-sample binary hand case) and its double redundancy against the code's own algebra only. The MATLAB reference `phyid` pins (pmediano/PhiID, commit a633cc1) implements MMI only, so no reference implementation of the double redundancy was available. In the revision after the second review Definition 1 was implemented directly (`notes/partB6_ccs_definition.py`) and run beside `phyid`'s mask on the same samples, at W = 60 and the global fit, both variants. The reading was fixed in the record before the run: the marginal pointwise mutual informations are the four between one past and one future variable, and the full pointwise mutual information is that of the joint past and joint future. The rule, fixed in advance, was that the two "agree on these data" if every whole-brain CCS-sts level and primary difference-in-differences (DiD; The DMT contrast, below) differs by less than 0.001 nats and no sign-flip p crosses 0.05, and that otherwise every CCS number in the paper is the published-definition value. They differed (section 1 of this text); all CCS values in this paper are under the published definition, with `phyid`'s beside them in `notes/review_results/partB/ccs_pub_tables.md`. The journal version (Mediano et al., 2025, SI Appendix, Definition 2) retains the definition verbatim on the sign condition, the samples "for which all marginal pointwise mutual informations, as well as the pointwise full mutual information I(X; Y), have the same sign" (checked 15 September 2026). CCS is computed here, as in `phyid`, with respect to the fitted Gaussian of the four-vector, p(x, y), which is what the SI Appendix of Mediano et al. (2025) states for their own results. For two Gaussian sources this is also Ince's published construction: the constraints of his maximum-entropy distribution P̂ (Ince, 2017, Definition 2 — both source–target marginals and the joint source marginal) fix every entry of the covariance, so P̂ is the fitted Gaussian and no projection arises; the conditionally independent distribution of his earlier construction (Ince, 2017, Definition 3) would differ and is not used.

**The family with lagged coupling.** The AR(1) family has no lagged interaction by construction, so its statements about q are statements about instantaneous correlation only. To see what interaction does, the symmetric VAR(1) pair x_{t+1} = a x_t + c y_t + ε, y_{t+1} = a y_t + c x_t + η, corr(ε, η) = q_ε, was evaluated in closed form (stationary covariance from the discrete Lyapunov equation; `notes/partB8_coupling_map.py`, `notes/review_results/partB/coupling_map_tables.md`). a and q_ε were re-solved for each c so that the pair's r₁ and q stay at the operating point. This was added in the revision after the second review, with no prediction recorded (record, "Part B, items 6–8"). At (0.85, 0.25), sts changes with c at fixed (r₁, q) with slope −1.77 nats per unit c and curvature +40 nats per unit c². The six cross-prediction atoms stay at zero for every c in this symmetric family. sts − (xtx + yty) equals rtr only at c = 0; for c > 0 it falls below rtr (at c = +0.02, sts − (xtx + yty) = +0.005 while rtr = 0.028) and for c < 0 it exceeds rtr (at c = −0.02, +0.059 against rtr = 0.019). The population cross-lag correlation departs from a_y q by ≈ 0.94c. At that point the table's rows give sts = 1.2315 and 1.3023 at c = +0.02 and −0.02, and 1.2155 and 1.4111 at c = +0.05 and −0.05, against 1.2588 at c = 0 (`coupling_map_tables.md`). Lagged interaction of |c| = 0.02 therefore moves sts at fixed (r₁, q) by amounts that ∂sts/∂r₁ ≈ 6 at the same point converts to a change in r₁ of the order of a hundredth, and |c| = 0.05 to a few hundredths, and the map's q-axis should not be read as "the contribution of interaction". No value of c is measured on the data; the family's rows are the only scale offered. A second process with the same signature is a pair sharing a slow component, x = s + n_x, y = ±s + n_y, with λ_x, λ_y the shares of var(s) in var(x) and var(y), and a_s and a_n the lag-1 autocorrelations of the shared and the regional parts (no AR(1) form is needed). Then q = ±√(λ_x λ_y), and the true cross-lag correlation exceeds the AR(1) substitution's a_y q by d(x→y) = q (1 − λ_y)(a_s − a_n), with equal loadings q (1 − |q|)(a_s − a_n). That excess is positive when the shared part is slower than the regional parts, and it follows the sign of q. The two are not separable at τ = 1: any positive-definite stationary 4 × 4 matrix of (x_t, y_t, x_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) is the lag-0/lag-1 structure of a VAR(1) with A = Γ₁Γ₀⁻¹, where Γ₁ = E[x_{t+1} x_tᵀ] and Γ₀ is the lag-0 covariance, and innovation covariance Γ₀ − AΓ₀Aᵀ, the Schur complement of the 4 × 4 matrix and so positive definite, which with Γ₀ ≻ 0 makes A stable (ρ(A) < 1) by the discrete Lyapunov theorem; so the sixteen τ = 1 atoms, the residual and CCS-sts of the two processes coincide in population. That is a population statement: a window's sample matrix has unequal diagonal blocks (mean |q_past − q_future| = 0.0168, `residual_source.log`). The worked example λ = 0.25, a_s = 0.95, a_n = 0.80 gives a = 0.8375, q = 0.25, cross-lag correlation 0.2375 and d = +0.028125, exactly the matrix of the symmetric family above with coefficient 0.83, c = 0.03 and q_ε = 0.093 (d = c(1 − q²); `notes/partB12_crosslag_budget.py`, population check).

## 4. The scope map, the overlay, the within-window regression and the regional maps

The scope-map tables are `partB/scope_map_tables.md` and `notes/partB1_scope_map.md` (record, "Part B, item 1"). On the AR(1) family sts is even in q, decreasing in |q| and steeply increasing in r₁ (main text, Fig 2a): at q = 0 it is 0.041 (r₁ = 0.2), 0.446 (0.6), 1.022 (0.8), 1.282 (0.85), 1.661 (0.9), 2.328 (0.95). At r₁ = 0.85 it falls from 1.282 to 1.131 as |q| goes from 0 to 0.6 (`notes/review_results/partB/scope_map_tables.md`). |∂sts/∂r₁| exceeds |∂sts/∂q| at every grid point except the line r₁ = 0: on the requested grid, on |q| ≤ 0.95 (0 of 18,336 grid points) and on r ∈ [−0.95, 0.95]² (0 of 36,481). The family therefore has no region in which q contributes comparably, and the fact that all of a subject's pairs fall in the r₁-dominated region carries no information about the pairs. What the overlay establishes is where the pairs sit and how large the ratio is there (Fig 2a of the main text, which pools the overlay's pre-injection windows of `ts_gsr`; `scripts/15_figures_v2.py`). At the operating point (0.85, 0.25), used for all derivative values quoted in this paper, the derivatives are 6.07 and −0.19 nats per unit (ratio 32); at (0.85, 0.6) they are 5.71 and −0.59 (10). The ratio's minimum is 6.55 at (0.61, ±0.6) on the grid |q| ≤ 0.6 and 1.9 at (0.77, ±0.95) on |q| ≤ 0.95 (`manuscript/figures/captions_v2.md`), so the two inputs approach parity only at cross-correlations far above those of BOLD region pairs. For subject 1 (both variants, both runs, windows 1–4 and 6) pair r₁ medians are 0.816–0.867 and median |q| 0.22–0.41 (`notes/partB1_scope_map.md`); the lag-1 autocorrelation of ideal band-passed white noise at the band-pass and TR settings of the published studies whose settings are stated is 0.78–0.93 (`notes/partB5_literature.md`, Table B; `notes/review_results/partB/family_checks.log`). Real BOLD has its power at the low end of the band — the in-band spectral centroid of the released series before injection is 0.0369 Hz on the DMT run and 0.0374 Hz on the placebo run, in a band of 0.01–0.08 Hz (`notes/review_results/logs/rev_extra.log`) — so these are lower bounds, not locations (here the ideal value is 0.82 against a measured 0.866); at those lower bounds the derivative ratio is 25–60 at |q| = 0.25, and it rises with r₁ (131 at 0.97). The same dependence is visible pair by pair within a single window: across the 6,555 pairs of subject 1, MMI-sts correlates with pair r₁ at r = +0.742 (DMT window 6) and +0.697 (placebo window 2), so about half of the between-pair variance of sts in a window (r² = 0.55, 0.49) is accounted for by lag-1 autocorrelation, and the prediction from each pair's (a_x, a_y, q) accounts for R² = 0.796 of it on average over windows (`ccs_pub_tables.md`; `diag_tables.md`). Under CCS the same correlations are −0.011 and −0.018 (main text, Results 6).

The regional test of the spatial-map claim, a computation added in the revision after the plain-language companion, with its prediction recorded before it was run (record, "Run-level mean cross-lag deviation and the regional sts–r₁ test", 15 September 2026; `notes/partB11_regional_sts_r1.py`), asks the same question region by region on the placebo run before injection: regional MMI-sts — the mean over a region's 114 pairs of the global-fit local sts, bins 1–8, `ts_gsr`, from the saved regional atoms of `scripts/11_regional_analysis.py` — against the region's lag-1 autocorrelation (windowed, W = 60, windows 1–4), both averaged over the 14 subjects. The prediction was a positive correlation if the spatial-map exposure is real on these data. ## 5. The DMT contrast: collinearity, leverage and the CCS contrast

The sts contrast is largely the autocorrelation contrast, with a remainder. Per subject the MMI-sts DiD and the lag-1 autocorrelation DiD correlate at Pearson r = 0.953 (Spearman 0.903) on `ts_gsr` and 0.96 (0.93) on `ts_demean` (main text, Fig 3a). Dropping every pair of subjects in turn (91 refits) gives r = 0.846 to 0.973 on `ts_gsr`, median 0.954, the minimum without subjects 8 and 14, the two farthest from the cloud (`notes/review_results/partB/leave_two_out.log`). Across the 28 condition × window group means the two series correlate at 0.977 and 0.938 (review, section 1; `ccs_pub_tables.md`). The autocorrelation contrast has the sts contrast's structure in every respect examined: the placebo run's own r₁ rises across the session and carries 0.34 of the DiD (sts: 0.40), the early set is larger (−0.0213, p = 0.0006) than the late (−0.0093, p = 0.1615), and both survive the trend corrections. Two facts keep the two contrasts from being identical. First, r = 0.953 leaves 9 % of the per-subject variance of the sts DiD unshared with the autocorrelation DiD; the diagnostic's residual DiD (main text, Results 4), a group-level quantity computed pair by pair, is a different remainder. Second, under the temporal null the sts DiD sits at 3.8 null SD (phase p = 0.0020) while the autocorrelation DiD sits at 1.8 (p = 0.0729), a difference the phase-randomised surrogate of a bounded, window-standardised statistic does not by itself explain. Neither fact bears on what the shared 91 % contains. Both DiDs are read off the same 60-TR windows, and at the split-half reliabilities of section 6 (0.72 and 0.74 for a half, 0.84 and 0.85 at full length by the Spearman–Brown formula) two measurements with independent errors could correlate at no more than about 0.84 (point estimates at N = 14), so part of the 0.953 is estimation error the two share. The collinearity therefore shows that the estimator's output follows each subject's measured r₁, which is the account; it is not evidence that the subjects' underlying drug effects on sts and on r₁ covary, and whether the measured r₁ contrast is itself a drug effect is that contrast's own inference (main text, Results 2).

## 6. The residual diagnostic: the finite-sample null, the cross-lag deviation and its budget, the directed component, and the split-half test

Finite-sample bias of the windowed estimator was characterised before any windowed result on simulated VAR(1) processes with analytic ground truth (`02_bias_check.py`, 2,000 replicate windows per cell). The estimated between-condition sts difference had the true sign in every cell, with 52–77 % of the true difference absorbed at W = 30 and 15–45 % at W = 60, which is why W = 60 became the primary window with W = 30 as a positive control (record, "Pre-registered analysis choices"). The real regional autocorrelation function decays faster than either simulated family, so no simulated shrinkage figure is applied to the real contrast; the calibration on the band-passed generator (section 7) is the estimate that uses the data's own spectrum. The sts-matched null (a post hoc computation; `notes/rev_sts_matched_null.py`) asked whether a covariance change at equal analytic sts can manufacture a difference. It used three families (the record's asymmetric VAR(1); a VAR(1) at a = 0.87; Gaussian processes with the data's own placebo and post-DMT autocorrelation functions), which yield four matched pairs. In the first family these are a lowered autocorrelation with the cross-coupling re-solved (F1-i) and a raised noise correlation with the autocorrelation re-solved (F1-ii). In the second only the noise-correlation shift can be matched (F2-ii), since no cross-coupling or noise correlation restores the analytic sts after a 0.02 drop in a. In the third the DMT-post spectrum was tilted to restore the placebo r₁ (F3-b), matching by q alone yielding no pair. The second condition's parameters were solved so that the analytic sts is unchanged, and the difference was estimated over 4,000 independent windows at W = 30, 60 and 840, as mean(shifted) − mean(baseline) with its standard error and as a share of the real primary DiD, −0.0809 (review, section 5). The decomposition was repeated with the lag-τ four-vector (x_t, y_t, x_{t+τ}, y_{t+τ}) for τ = 2, 3, 5 on `ts_gsr`, windowed and global, with the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast built in the same way; the analysis plan recorded beforehand was that the lag-τ autocorrelation r_τ falls with τ and the artefact weakens (section 9).

The residual DiD is positive in every variant × estimator combination (main text, Table 4: +0.0115 [+0.0021, +0.0211] at the primary one). It survives FD residualisation and is concentrated in the drug-present windows (early set +0.0179 [+0.0099, +0.0260], p = 0.0012, phase p = 0.003, positive in 12 of 14; late set +0.0064, p = 0.32). Within each run alone the change is not significant: DMT post − pre +0.0077 [−0.0001, +0.0149], p = 0.080; placebo −0.0038 [−0.0091, +0.0014], p = 0.19; the placebo run contributes 0.33 of the DiD (`notes/review_results/inference_rows_diag.csv`), so the DiD's significance rests on the two runs moving in opposite directions, a third of it on the placebo run. Under the reading of earlier drafts — the plan's "near zero" branch read as a residual DiD whose subject-bootstrap 95 % CI includes zero (a reading stated in the record's entry of 15 September 2026, 15:46 UTC, not in the plan) — neither of the plan's two branches obtained: the CI [+0.0021, +0.0211] excludes zero, and the residual is not uncorrelated with r₁ (the subjects whose r₁ fell more show the larger over-prediction, r = −0.780). The calibration of the main text (Results 4) replaces that reading: the reference for the residual DiD is its finite-sample expectation, not zero. A post-hoc supplement (`ts_gsr` W = 60; `notes/review_results/partB/residual_source.log`) traced the level residual to the diagnostic's single model assumption, corr(x_t, y_{t+1}) = a_y q. The cross-lag substitution alone reproduces it (−0.0489 of −0.0489). The measured cross-lag correlations scatter about a_y q with a signed mean offset of +0.00014 (a signed mean, in which pairs of opposite q cancel; see below), and the residual is more negative where pair a and |q| are higher (R² = 0.74 on pair a, pair |q| and the cross-lag scatter). Those correlations cannot decide whether the positive residual DiD is that over-prediction shrinking as a and |q| fall under DMT or a component that scales with the drug effect, because both predict them. The finite-sample null (a computation of the second review, with no pre-recorded rule; its construction below) can, within the limits of its construction. With the data's own autocorrelation function, pair heterogeneity and the four cells' operating points, it gives a residual level of −0.035 (−3.0 %) against the observed −0.049 (−4.3 %) and a residual DiD of +0.0054 against the observed +0.0115, reproducing the DMT-run change (+0.0041 against +0.0077) and not the placebo-run change (−0.0013 against −0.0038). It gives a level of −8.5 %, −3.1 % and −0.34 % at W = 30, 60 and 840 against the data's −9.7 %, −4.3 % and −1.1 % (`notes/review_results/logs/review_v2_residual_null.log`). That value depends on choices the null leaves free: in the third review's re-run, per-pair filter heterogeneity of 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 of the mean gives a null DiD of +0.0059, +0.0052 and +0.0077, and applying the placebo autocorrelation shape to every cell gives +0.0037 (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_second_pass_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix, computation 3). So the null accounts for a third to two-thirds of the observed residual DiD, and the observed interval contains every one of those values; the excess over the null is not distinguishable from zero at any of the null's values (exact sign-flip p = 0.15 at +0.0037, 0.25 at +0.0054 and 0.47 at +0.0077, each value treated as fixed). The calibration on generators with a known change (section 7; main text, Results 4) supersedes this null where the two overlap. What does exceed the null is the run-level residual on `ts_gsr`, −0.0137 [−0.0145, −0.0130], negative in 14 of 14 subjects, against the null's −0.0045 (−1.1 % against −0.34 %; sign-flip p = 0.0001; like the p values above, a computation of the fresh review, `notes/fresh_review_2026-09-17/checks/check_C1_residual_vs_null.log`, with no pre-recorded rule); on `ts_demean` the run-level residual is +0.1 %, i.e. absent (main text, Table 4). That null is stationary, and the runs are not, by design: the DMT run carries an injection at TR 240 and the placebo run an r₁ and variance drift across the session (window variance / run variance 1.28 → 0.89 on DMT, 0.94 → 1.06 on placebo; review, section 6).

**The cross-lag deviation and its budget.** For each pair, d is the measured lag-1 cross-correlation minus the substitution's a_y q (both directions averaged), and δ_run its mean over pairs weighted by s, the sign of the pair's run-level q, so that a positive value is a departure in the direction that lowers sts. Over the run's 14 windows of W = 60 (n_w TRs, SDs σ; π_w ∝ n_w σ_{x,w} σ_{y,w}, ψ_w ∝ n_w σ²_{y,w}, ā_y = Σ_w ψ_w a_{y,w}), δ_run is written as the sum of four terms:

δ_run = δ_within + δ_pool + δ_means + ε,   δ_within = s Σ_w π_w d_w,   δ_pool = s Σ_w π_w (a_{y,w} − ā_y) q_w,   δ_means = δ_run − δ_wd,

with δ_wd the statistic after each region's mean is removed within each window, and ε = δ_wd − δ_within − δ_pool the closing remainder, which collects the effects of window boundaries and standardisation (`notes/rev_crosslag_budget.py`, `partB12_crosslag_budget.py`, `partB13_crosslag_budget_null.py`). The identity is therefore exact by construction, δ_within and δ_pool being the two terms built independently; |ε| is 0.00002 on `ts_gsr` and 0.00005 on `ts_demean` (grand means; `notes/review_results/partB/crosslag_budget_tables.md`). For the budget the finite-sample null described above was simulated as 840-TR runs per run type, with its mean filter parameter β̄ and a two-component mixture for q solved to the data's run-level mean a, mean |q̂| and fraction |q̂| < 0.05. Six configurations were run (heterogeneity 0.5, 0.25, 1.0; placebo ACF for both runs; Gaussian q; window-level targets), ≥ 25,000 pair-runs each. Two controls, a shared slow component (λ = 0.2, a_s − a_n = 0.10) and pooling (q and filter changing at TR 240), were scored the same way. Null-corrected terms are data minus null; the reading rules were recorded before the run (record, "The cross-lag budget: pre-run entry", 16 September 2026, 16:24 UTC).

The diagnostic's one assumption, corr(x_t, y_{t+1}) = a_y q, was tested on the cross-lag correlations themselves. Their departure from it, d, lowers sts where q > 0 and raises it where q < 0 (reversing one region's sign reverses both and changes no atom). A mechanism acting through the cross-lag correlations therefore shows in δ_run, the mean of d over pairs weighted by the sign of the pair's run-level q, so that a positive value is a departure in the direction that lowers sts; a signed mean cancels on `ts_gsr`, where 54.5 % of pairs have q < 0. At the run level δ_run = +0.00340 [+0.00300, +0.00380], 14 of 14 subjects (DMT run +0.00381, placebo +0.00299): on the family's scale 44 % of the −0.0137 run-level residual at the operating point, 37 % at the pairs' run-level mean point (a 0.8666, |q| 0.1945), a scale rather than a decomposition. Four accounts leave that signature: finite sampling; pooling of windows that differ in a, q or variance under one fit; lagged coupling; and a slow component shared by the two regions, d = q(1 − λ_y)(a_s − a_n) (section 3 of this text). The last two are one account at τ = 1, every stationary lag-0/lag-1 structure being that of a VAR(1) with A = Γ₁Γ₀⁻¹; the worked example (λ = 0.25, a_s = 0.95, a_n = 0.80) is the coupled family at coefficient 0.83, c = 0.03, q_ε = 0.093 (section 3). The exact budget δ_run = δ_within + δ_pool + δ_means + ε (above) was read under rules recorded before the run, against a finite-sample null solved to the data (six configurations) and two controls. Finite sampling is a minor part: the null's δ_run is +0.00029 to +0.00046, 8–14 % of the observed δ_run (the null's value depends on the density of q̂ near zero, to which each configuration is solved; above). Of the null-corrected +0.00301 [+0.00261, +0.00340], the within-window term is most (+0.00250, 83–84 % in every configuration; a shared-slow-component control puts 90 % there, a pooling control 30 %, and in that control the within-window term equals the null's (+0.00040), i.e. it is finite sampling), pooling a minor part (+0.00046, 15 %) and the window means a minor part (+0.00004). Pooling is larger on the DMT run (DMT − placebo +0.00039 per subject, sign-flip p = 0.0006, 12 of 14; 11 % of that run's +0.00349). So the departure is present inside 2-minute windows, where pooling across windows cannot act; pooling is not its principal account, and the DMT run carries about a tenth more of it. On `ts_demean` δ_run is −0.00262 [−0.00495, −0.00037]; its sign reversal without global signal regression is an unknown (main text, Discussion). What acts inside the windows — common slow drive or lagged coupling, non-stationarity faster than two minutes, or a within-window finite-sample effect the null as specified does not reproduce — is not identified (S9 Table; record, "The cross-lag budget: outcome", 16 September 2026); the run-level residual itself is located by the directed and symmetric components above (B15).

Two additions were made in the revision after the second review, with rules recorded before they were run (record, "Part B, items 6–8", pre-run entry of 15 September, 07:30 UTC): the CCS definition check (above) and a split-half test of the per-subject correlation between the residual DiD and the CCS-sts DiD (`notes/partB7_splithalf.py`). In the split-half test both DiDs were recomputed on odd windows only (pre 1, 3; post 7, 9, 11, 13) and on even windows only (pre 2, 4; post 6, 8, 10, 12, 14), and the within-half correlations were compared with the cross-half ones, which shared window noise cannot produce. The rule, fixed in advance on `ts_gsr`, was: not window noise if both cross-half correlations are positive and their mean is at least half the within-half mean. In that case the two are reported together as one exploratory observation of an autocorrelation-independent component whose sign matches the original pre-specified direction, with the statement that it arose after four specification changes and requires an independent dataset. Estimation noise if the mean cross-half correlation is below half the within-half mean or either is negative; in that case both are reported as null with the test as the evidence. That rule was set without reference to the split-half reliabilities of the two quantities, and at the reliabilities observed its pass criterion lay above the largest cross-half correlation two perfectly correlated reliable components could produce, so the test could not discriminate. The flaw is recorded as such (record, correction note of 15 September) and the outcome is reported as undetermined (section 6 of this text). A third computation, the finite-sample null of the residual (`notes/review_v2_residual_null.py`), is not an addition of ours: it was written and run by the second adversarial review, before any record entry and with no recorded rule (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix B), and is reported here as a post hoc computation. In it each pair is one linear filter applied to two white noises with correlation q, so the population cross-lag correlation is exactly r₁q and every residual is finite-sample. The filter (band-pass 0.01–0.08 Hz times exp(−βf²)) is fitted to the pooled placebo autocorrelation function (lags 1–6 fitted 0.872, 0.549, 0.179, −0.091, −0.196, −0.168). For the DMT-post cell it is fitted to the post-injection DMT function. β and q vary across pairs so that the window-level mean pair a and |q| equal the four run × period cells of `residual_source.log` (DMT and placebo, pre-injection and primary windows); 3,000 pairs × 50 windows per cell, a cell being here one of those run × period combinations. The null is stationary by construction. The third review re-ran it (it reproduces) and varied two of its free choices, the per-pair heterogeneity of the filter and whether the DMT-post autocorrelation shape is applied; the paper quotes the range those variations give (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_second_pass_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix, computation 3). One technicality of the residual's inference: its phase-randomised null randomises the observed TR-resolution series against the fixed per-window prediction, so it is a null of the observed series' temporal structure rather than of the residual's construction; the sign-flip test and the bootstrap carry the residual inference.

The residual's per-subject variance is shared with CCS-sts. Across subjects the residual DiD (W = 60) correlates with the CCS-sts DiD at r = +0.799 on `ts_gsr` at W = 60, +0.094 at the `ts_gsr` global fit, +0.875 on `ts_demean` at W = 60 and +0.676 at the `ts_demean` global fit (`ccs_pub_tables.md`). Fig 3c of the main text shows the W = 60 pair on `ts_gsr`. The relation survives partialling out the autocorrelation DiD (partial r = +0.83 and +0.86 under the published CCS definition, computed by the third review from the committed per-subject DiDs, `notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_second_pass_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix, computation 4; `phyid`'s mask gives +0.69 and +0.82). Both are functions of the same per-window 4 × 4 matrices, so the shared variance may be common signal or common estimation noise. The split-half test (the rule above; the table below; `notes/review_results/partB/splithalf_tables.md`) was meant to separate them: on `ts_gsr` the within-half correlations are +0.847 and +0.783 (mean +0.815) and the cross-half correlations +0.160 and +0.478 (mean +0.319, ratio 0.39; after partialling out the same-half autocorrelation DiD +0.815 within against +0.154 across); on `ts_demean` +0.881 within against +0.283 across (0.32). Under the rule fixed before the test the mean cross-half correlation (0.32) fell below half the within-half mean (0.41), the branch the rule labels estimation noise. The rule cannot carry that label. The split-half reliabilities of the two quantities are low (residual DiD 0.49, CCS-sts DiD 0.30 on `ts_gsr`; 0.22 and 0.42 on `ts_demean`; for comparison the autocorrelation and MMI-sts DiDs 0.71–0.74 and 0.69–0.72). So the largest cross-half correlation two perfectly correlated reliable components could show is √(0.494 × 0.302) = 0.39 on `ts_gsr` (0.30 on `ts_demean`, against a threshold of 0.44 there). The rule's pass criterion of 0.41 lay above that ceiling: its "not window noise" branch was unreachable at these reliabilities, the rule was set without reference to the ceiling, and that is a flaw in the rule, recorded as such (record, correction note of 15 September). What the numbers say is this. The observed cross-half correlation of 0.32 is 0.82 of the ceiling (its disattenuated value), which is consistent with a shared reliable component; the reliabilities are too low to distinguish that from a partial one, or, at N = 14 (cross-half p = 0.59 and 0.08), from none. The verdict is undetermined. The test does establish that most of the within-half correlation (0.815 against a ceiling of 0.39) is estimation noise common to the same windows and that the reliable part of each quantity is small. The group-mean DiDs of both quantities are positive in both halves (residual +0.0067 / +0.0164; CCS-sts +0.0033 / +0.0054). No autocorrelation-independent component is reported: the residual and the CCS-sts change remain exploratory, they arose after four specification changes (windowed sts → sixteen atoms → CCS → diagnostic residual), and a pre-specified test on an independent dataset is what would settle whether their shared component is signal. The relation between the residual and the windowed estimator's manufacture (section 9 of this text; main text, Results 7) is the same question from the other side: both are the part of the windowed DiD that the family does not account for. The run-level residual (`ts_gsr`, −1.1 % against the null's −0.34 %) is the estimate least exposed to manufacture and the one the non-stationarity of the runs bears on most.

## 9. Manufacture and the lag variants

Where an sts-matched pair of conditions can be built (equal analytic sts, different covariance), the windowed estimator manufactures a difference at W = 60 of −9 % (F1-i), +16 % (F1-ii), +7 % ± 8 % (F2-ii) and −8 % ± 6 % (F3-b) of the real primary DiD of −0.0809, of either sign; the ± figures are the standard errors over the 4,000 windows of the two pairs whose difference is not distinguishable from zero (review, section 5). At W = 30 it manufactures up to +33 %. At the full run length it is ≤ 2 % except in one matched pair (F2-ii, +29 %) (review, section 5; a post hoc computation). A matched pair in which r₁ falls by the observed amount could not be built at data-like autocorrelation, because the analytic sts is a steep function of r₁ (≈ +0.07 nats per +0.01 near 0.87) and a weak function of q. The observed autocorrelation-function change alone, at fixed q, changes the true sts by −0.069 against the observed DiD of −0.081, and the W = 60 estimator returns −0.056 of it. This is the third figure the paper gives for the sts change that the autocorrelation change produces, beside the map projection (−0.087) and the per-pair prediction (−0.0924) of the main text's Table 5. The three answer three questions: the mean r₁ change passed through the slope at one point; each pair's own (a_x, a_y, q) averaged over pairs; and the change in the true sts under the whole autocorrelation-function change, before the estimator's finite-sample shrinkage. All three have the observed sign, and the differences between them are the residual and the manufacture figures seen from different sides: the diagnostic's over-shoot of the observed DiD is the residual DiD of the main text's Results 4, and the gap between the true change (−0.069) and what the estimator returns of it (−0.056) is the shrinkage characterised above. The windowed whole-brain sts sits 0.1531 nats below the global fit in the DMT pre-injection windows (1.1554 against 1.3085); the diagnostic decomposes that gap into the lower within-window parameters, which the prediction from the window's own (a_x, a_y, q) captures, and a residual of −0.0489 against −0.0137 at the run level. On manufacture grounds the global fit is the better estimator for a contrast between separately fitted runs or groups: at 840 samples it manufactures ≤ 2 % in three of the four matched pairs (+29 % in the fourth) and its run-level residual is 1.1 % on `ts_gsr` (0.1 % on `ts_demean`). Two things qualify that recommendation, and it rests on the manufacture figures alone. First, the global fit's per-bin values are local atoms under a single run-level covariance, not per-bin decompositions. A pre/post contrast within a run — the contrast this paper runs — is therefore exposed to the within-run variance change that the main text's Results 7 gives as a reason for caution with the CCS global-fit values. On the DMT run the ratio of window variance to run variance goes from 1.28 to 0.89, and the global-fit sts bins correlate with that ratio at +0.37 per run on average (review, section 1). The windowed series follow the same ratio more closely — windowed sts at +0.49 and window-standardised r₁ at +0.54 per run on average, +0.77 and +0.73 on the group-mean series (review, section 6) — but not by that route: each window is standardised and fitted on its own samples, so its variance level cannot enter its atoms, and the correlation records that in these runs variance falls in the windows where r₁ falls. Variance can move the windowed contrast only through the window's correlation matrix, which the diagnostic models; partialling the ratio out of the relation across the 28 group-mean windows leaves r = +0.95 (from +0.97 on the series as printed to three decimals, 0.977 unrounded, section 5 of this text; a computation of the fresh review, `notes/fresh_review_2026-09-17/checks/check_C2_variance_ratio.log`). No contrast is residualised on the ratio: for the windowed estimator that would remove the part of the r₁ change that coincides with the variance change, not an artefact of scale. The global-fit MMI-sts contrast (−0.0801 against −0.0809 at W = 60) is therefore quoted as agreement between two estimators, not as an independent confirmation. Second, the global fit on `ts_gsr` is the one cell of four in which sts fell less than proportionally to TDMI (main text, Results 2, +0.0204, p = 0.025), which unequal changes of a_x and a_y explain; the recommendation is not made on that ground. The windowed W = 60 estimator remains this paper's primary because it was the pre-specified one and the two agree.

The main text's Table 6 and Fig 5 give the lag variants (`ts_gsr`, W = 60; `notes/review_results/partB/lag_tables.md`). As the plan anticipated, r_τ falls with τ and the artefact weakens: the sts contrast is significant on both nulls at both estimators wherever the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast is significant by sign-flip (τ = 1, 2, 3; the sts contrast's phase-randomised p = 0.0020, 0.0010 and 0.0010 at W = 60 and 0.0010 at the global fit; the autocorrelation contrast's is 0.0729, 0.0040 and 0.0020, so at τ = 1 it misses that null) and null where it is not (τ = 5; W = 60 p = 0.3688, phase p = 0.2607; global fit +0.0015, p = 0.7997, phase p = 0.7692). Per subject it tracks the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast, with the sign reversed at τ = 5 because r₅ is negative and every AR(1) atom is even in the autocorrelation. At τ = 3 the tracking is only partial: the per-subject correlation is 0.626 at W = 60 (0.885 at the global fit) while the sts contrast is significant on both nulls and survives FD residualisation. So at τ = 3 the sts contrast is only partly the r₃ contrast, which is either a limit of the map at low r_τ (r₃ = 0.14, where ∂sts/∂r is small and the estimator's scatter is a larger share of the atom) or a second remainder; the data do not say which. The lag-τ autocorrelation contrast itself grows with τ (−0.0146, −0.0468, −0.0681 at τ = 1, 2, 3; main text, Table 6). Relative to the sts level at each lag the sts contrast is −7 %, −24 % and −16 % at τ = 1, 2, 3 (−0.0809 of 1.1554, −0.0429 of 0.1797, −0.0043 of 0.0262; main text, Table 6), so in relative terms it does not weaken; and the bootstrap intervals of the three autocorrelation contrasts overlap ([−0.0251, −0.0052], [−0.0754, −0.0174], [−0.1120, −0.0213]; `inference_rows_lag.csv`), so their growth with τ is a statement about point estimates. DMT changes the shape of the regional autocorrelation function towards a faster decay, of which the r₁ change is the lag-1 summary, and the in-band spectral centroid — the power-weighted mean frequency within 0.01–0.08 Hz, a post hoc computation (`rev_extra.py`) — rises accordingly (+0.0023 Hz [+0.0006, +0.0040], p = 0.022, 12 of 14 on `ts_gsr`; review, section 1). The paper does not interpret the shape change beyond this: no pre-specified quantity addresses it, and the τ = 1 estimator the literature uses sees only its lag-1 summary. It is stated so that "DMT lowers r₁" is not taken for the whole of the effect on the autocorrelation function, and it is one candidate for why the τ = 3 sts contrast tracks r₃ only partly. No lag moves the pairs to a point where q contributes comparably, because no such point exists on the family at BOLD cross-correlations.
