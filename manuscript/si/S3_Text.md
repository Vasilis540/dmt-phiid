## 2. The CCS definition check

Under MMI each single-target redundancy is the smaller of the two single-source mutual informations (Barrett, 2015) and the double redundancy rtr is the smallest of the four single-source, single-target mutual informations (Mediano et al., 2021, Appendix, Definition 2). Under CCS (Ince, 2017) a single-target redundancy is the local co-information at the samples where the constituent local mutual informations and the co-information share a sign, and zero elsewhere. The ΦID double redundancy under CCS is defined by Mediano et al. (2021, Appendix, Definition 1; 2025, SI Appendix, Definition 2) as the pointwise double co-information c(x; y), summed over the samples at which all marginal pointwise mutual informations and the pointwise full mutual information i(x; y) share a sign. The double co-information is the signed sum, an inclusion–exclusion over the product lattice, of the pointwise redundancy function at its fifteen nodes other than the bottom node rtr: the local mutual information at the nine nodes whose past and future sets are each a single set, and the local single-target CCS redundancy (Ince, 2017) at the six nodes with two separate past or two separate future variables (Mediano et al., 2021, Appendix, Eq. 5; 2025, SI Appendix, Eq. 6). `phyid` implements a different mask: it keeps c at the samples where the four single-source, single-target local mutual informations and c itself share a sign, so its fifth sign condition is that of the double co-information rather than of the full mutual information. On the lattice c ≡ rtr − sts (deviation ≤ 3 × 10⁻¹⁵; the SI Appendix of Mediano et al., 2025, states the same identity, c(x; y) = I∂^{{1}{2}→{1}{2}} − I∂^{{12}→{12}}), so under either mask CCS-sts is 0 at the selected samples and −c elsewhere, and its whole-brain value is the average of that quantity over all samples. An earlier check (`notes/partB2_ccs.md`) had verified `phyid`'s single-target CCS against an independent implementation of Ince's construction (agreement 2.1 × 10⁻¹³ over 200 random draws of the real data; 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ on a 56-sample binary hand case) and its double redundancy against the code's own algebra only. The MATLAB reference `phyid` pins (pmediano/PhiID, commit a633cc1) implements MMI only, so no reference implementation of the double redundancy was available. In the revision after the second review (15 September 2026) Definition 1 was implemented directly (`notes/partB6_ccs_definition.py`) and run beside `phyid`'s mask on the same samples, at W = 60 and the global fit, both variants. The reading was fixed in the record before the run: the marginal pointwise mutual informations are the four between one past and one future variable, and the full pointwise mutual information is that of the joint past and joint future. The rule, fixed in advance, was that the two "agree on these data" if every whole-brain CCS-sts level and primary difference-in-differences (DiD; The DMT contrast, below) differs by less than 0.001 nats and no sign-flip p crosses 0.05, and that otherwise every CCS number in the paper is the published-definition value. They differed (section 1 of this text); all CCS values in this paper are under the published definition, with `phyid`'s beside them in `notes/review_results/partB/ccs_pub_tables.md`. The journal version (Mediano et al., 2025, SI Appendix, Definition 2) retains the definition verbatim on the sign condition, the samples "for which all marginal pointwise mutual informations, as well as the pointwise full mutual information I(X; Y), have the same sign" (checked 15 September 2026). CCS is computed here, as in `phyid`, with respect to the fitted Gaussian of the four-vector, p(x, y), which is what the SI Appendix of Mediano et al. (2025) states for their own results. For two Gaussian sources this is also Ince's published construction: the constraints of his maximum-entropy distribution P̂ (Ince, 2017, Definition 2 — both source–target marginals and the joint source marginal) fix every entry of the covariance, so P̂ is the fitted Gaussian and no projection arises; the conditionally independent distribution of his earlier construction (Ince, 2017, Definition 3) would differ and is not used.

## 3. The closed form: checks, the coupled family, and the τ = 1 non-separability argument

On the symmetric family the pair's entire TDMI is self-prediction, and MMI assigns all of it but the cross-lag mutual information C to sts (this and the next two sentences moved from the main text on 23 September 2026). With S = −½ ln(1 − a²) and C = −½ ln(1 − a²q²), the block determinants of the 4 × 4 correlation matrix give I(x_t; x_{t+1}) = S, I(x_t; y_{t+1}) = C, every single-source joint-target and joint-source single-target mutual information S, and TDMI = 2S; MMI sets the four single-source, single-target redundancies to C, the two joint-target ones to S, and rtr = C, and the Möbius inversion gives the atoms of Results 1, with ∂sts/∂a = 2a/(1 − a²) − aq²/(1 − a²q²) and ∂sts/∂q = −a²q/(1 − a²q²). For unequal coefficients the same pipeline evaluates the matrix with cross-lag entries a_y q and a_x q numerically; the AR(1)-substituted atoms of Table 1 are these at each pair's measured (a_x, a_y, q), averaged over pairs. The identities of the main text's Results 1 were checked numerically to 4 × 10⁻¹⁵ over 2,000 seeded draws of (a, q) (`partB/family_checks.log`). The four mirror atoms are negative on the whole family; that MMI-ΦID can produce negative atoms is noted by its authors (Mediano et al., 2025, SI Appendix, Sec. III.A; Luppi et al., 2026), and under MMI the redundancy and synergy atoms are guaranteed non-negative while many of the others can take negative values (Down et al., 2026). The identity at q = 0 with unequal coefficients, sts = 2 min(S_x, S_y) and rtr = 0, holds to 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ at (a_x, a_y) = (0.83, 0.87), (0.85, 0.85), (0.8, 0.9) and (0.95, 0.6) (`partB/exchange_rates_tables.md`, part (a)). For q ≠ 0, B23 (f) (`partB/diagnostic_alternatives_tables.md`; S18 Table) evaluated the family on a grid of mean a 0.80, 0.85 and 0.90 × q 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.40, 0.50 and 0.70 × |a_x − a_y| from 0 to 0.10 in steps of 0.0005: str equals the smaller self-prediction atom to 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁶, rts − str is never below −2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶, and sts − (xtx + yty + rtr) never exceeds 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁶, its maximum at equal coefficients; the excess sts − (xtx + yty), which is rtr at equal coefficients, first turns negative at an asymmetry of 0.0080 at (0.85, 0.25), 0.0345 at q = 0.5 and 0.0915 at q = 0.7 (0.0005 at q = 0.05; at mean a 0.80 and q = 0.70 not within 0.10). **The family with lagged coupling.** The AR(1) family has no lagged interaction by construction, so its statements about q are statements about instantaneous correlation only. To see what interaction does, the symmetric VAR(1) pair x_{t+1} = a x_t + c y_t + ε, y_{t+1} = a y_t + c x_t + η, corr(ε, η) = q_ε, was evaluated in closed form (stationary covariance from the discrete Lyapunov equation; `notes/partB8_coupling_map.py`, `notes/review_results/partB/coupling_map_tables.md`). a and q_ε were re-solved for each c so that the pair's r₁ and q stay at the operating point. This was added in the revision after the second review, with no prediction recorded (record, "Part B, items 6–8"). At (0.85, 0.25), sts changes with c at fixed (r₁, q) with slope −1.74 nats per unit c (the fine central difference of `partB19`; the difference over the table's ±0.02 rows gives −1.77) and curvature +40 nats per unit c². The six cross-prediction atoms stay at zero for every c in this symmetric family. sts − (xtx + yty) equals rtr only at c = 0; for c > 0 it falls below rtr (at c = +0.02, sts − (xtx + yty) = +0.005 while rtr = 0.028) and for c < 0 it exceeds rtr (at c = −0.02, +0.059 against rtr = 0.019). The population cross-lag correlation departs from a_y q by ≈ 0.94c. At that point the table's rows give sts = 1.2315 and 1.3023 at c = +0.02 and −0.02, and 1.2155 and 1.4111 at c = +0.05 and −0.05, against 1.2588 at c = 0 (`coupling_map_tables.md`). The family is invariant under (c, q) → (−c, −q), so at q = −0.25 the same values hold with the sign of c reversed (1.3023 at c = +0.02, +0.0435 above c = 0): coupling of the same sign as q lowers sts, and coupling of the opposite sign raises it (main text, Fig 1c, whose dashed curve is the q = −0.25 case). The ΦID authors' own worked example is a coupled AR pair (Mediano et al., 2021, Fig. 5 and Appendix VI), the symmetric coupled family of this section with self- and cross-coefficient 0.4 (their c is the innovation correlation, which they vary); the family with zero cross-coefficient is the diagonal family used here. Lagged interaction of |c| = 0.02 therefore moves sts at fixed (r₁, q) by amounts that ∂sts/∂r₁ ≈ 6 at the same point converts to a change in r₁ of the order of a hundredth, and |c| = 0.05 to a few hundredths, and the map's q-axis should not be read as "the contribution of interaction". No value of c is measured on the data; the family's rows are the only scale offered. A second process with the same signature is a pair sharing a slow component, x = s + n_x, y = ±s + n_y, with λ_x, λ_y the shares of var(s) in var(x) and var(y), and a_s and a_n the lag-1 autocorrelations of the shared and the regional parts (no AR(1) form is needed). Then q = ±√(λ_x λ_y), and the true cross-lag correlation exceeds the AR(1) substitution's a_y q by d(x→y) = q (1 − λ_y)(a_s − a_n), with equal loadings q (1 − |q|)(a_s − a_n). That excess is positive when the shared part is slower than the regional parts, and it follows the sign of q. The two are not separable at τ = 1: any positive-definite stationary 4 × 4 matrix of (x_t, y_t, x_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) is the lag-0/lag-1 structure of a VAR(1) with A = Γ₁Γ₀⁻¹, where Γ₁ = E[x_{t+1} x_tᵀ] and Γ₀ is the lag-0 covariance, and innovation covariance Γ₀ − AΓ₀Aᵀ, the Schur complement of the 4 × 4 matrix and so positive definite, which with Γ₀ ≻ 0 makes A stable (ρ(A) < 1) by the discrete Lyapunov theorem; so the sixteen τ = 1 atoms, the residual and CCS-sts of the two processes coincide in population. That is a population statement: a window's sample matrix has unequal diagonal blocks (mean |q_past − q_future| = 0.0168, `residual_source.log`). The worked example λ = 0.25, a_s = 0.95, a_n = 0.80 gives a = 0.8375, q = 0.25, cross-lag correlation 0.2375 and d = +0.028125, exactly the matrix of the symmetric family above with coefficient 0.83, c = 0.03 and q_ε = 0.093 (d = c(1 − q²); `notes/partB12_crosslag_budget.py`, population check).

## 4. The scope map, the overlay, the within-window regression and the regional maps

The scope-map tables are `partB/scope_map_tables.md` and `notes/partB1_scope_map.md` (pre-specified as B1, with no prediction, in `notes/partB_prespec_2026-09-14.md`). On the AR(1) family sts is even in q, decreasing in |q| and steeply increasing in r₁ (main text, Fig 1a): at q = 0 it is 0.041 (r₁ = 0.2), 0.446 (0.6), 1.022 (0.8), 1.282 (0.85), 1.661 (0.9), 2.328 (0.95). ## 5. The DMT contrast: collinearity, leverage and the CCS contrast

The sts contrast is largely the autocorrelation contrast, with a remainder. Per subject the MMI-sts DiD and the lag-1 autocorrelation DiD correlate at Pearson r = 0.953 (Spearman 0.903) on `ts_gsr` and 0.96 (0.93) on `ts_demean` (main text, Fig 3a). Dropping every pair of subjects in turn (91 refits) gives r = 0.846 to 0.973 on `ts_gsr`, median 0.954, the minimum without subjects 8 and 14, the two farthest from the cloud (`notes/review_results/partB/leave_two_out.log`). Across the 28 condition × window group means the two series correlate at 0.977 and 0.938 (the review computations of 14 September 2026, `notes/review_computations_2026-09-14.md`, section 1; `ccs_pub_tables.md`). The autocorrelation contrast has the sts contrast's structure in every respect examined: the placebo run's own r₁ rises across the session and carries 0.34 of the DiD (sts: 0.40), the early set is larger (−0.0213, p = 0.0006) than the late (−0.0093, p = 0.1615), and under the two trend corrections the autocorrelation contrast is −0.0162 [−0.0271, −0.0056] and −0.0190 [−0.0290, −0.0089] (`inference_revision.csv`). The drift's source is not identified; lower arousal later in a session is one candidate, since BOLD lag-1 autocorrelation rises in propofol sedation (Huang et al., 2018). Two facts keep the two contrasts from being identical. First, r = 0.953 leaves 9 % of the per-subject variance of the sts DiD unshared with the autocorrelation DiD; the residual of the AR(1)-substituted estimate (main text, Results 4), a group-level quantity computed pair by pair, is a different remainder. Second, under the temporal null the sts DiD sits at 3.8 null SD (phase p = 0.0020) while the autocorrelation DiD sits at 1.8 (p = 0.0729), a difference the phase-randomised surrogate of a bounded, window-standardised statistic does not by itself explain. Neither fact bears on what the shared 91 % contains. Both DiDs are read off the same 60-TR windows, and at the split-half reliabilities of section 6 (0.72 and 0.74 for a half, 0.84 and 0.85 at full length by the Spearman–Brown formula) two measurements with independent errors could correlate at no more than about 0.84 (point estimates at N = 14), so part of the 0.953 is estimation error the two share. The collinearity therefore shows that the estimator's output follows each subject's measured r₁, which is the account; it is not evidence that the subjects' underlying drug effects on sts and on r₁ covary, and whether the measured r₁ contrast is itself a drug effect is that contrast's own inference (main text, Results 2).

## 6. The residual of the AR(1)-substituted estimate: the finite-sample null, the cross-lag deviation and its budget, the directed and aligned statistics, and the split-half test

Finite-sample bias of the windowed estimator was characterised before any windowed result on simulated VAR(1) processes with analytic ground truth (`02_bias_check.py`, 2,000 replicate windows per cell). The estimated between-condition sts difference had the true sign in every cell, with 52–77 % of the true difference absorbed at W = 30 and 15–45 % at W = 60, which is why W = 60 became the primary window with W = 30 as a positive control (record, "Pre-registered analysis choices"). The real regional autocorrelation function decays faster than either simulated family, so no simulated shrinkage figure is applied to the real contrast; the calibration on the band-passed generator (section 7) is the estimate that uses the data's own spectrum. The sts-matched null (a post hoc computation; `notes/rev_sts_matched_null.py`) asked whether a covariance change at equal analytic sts can manufacture a difference. It used three families (the record's asymmetric VAR(1); a VAR(1) at a = 0.87; Gaussian processes with the data's own placebo and post-DMT autocorrelation functions), which yield four matched pairs. In the first family these are a lowered autocorrelation with the cross-coupling re-solved (F1-i) and a raised noise correlation with the autocorrelation re-solved (F1-ii). In the second only the noise-correlation shift can be matched (F2-ii), since no cross-coupling or noise correlation restores the analytic sts after a 0.02 drop in a. In the third the DMT-post spectrum was tilted to restore the placebo r₁ (F3-b), matching by q alone yielding no pair. The second condition's parameters were solved so that the analytic sts is unchanged, and the difference was estimated over 4,000 independent windows at W = 30, 60 and 840, as mean(shifted) − mean(baseline) with its standard error and as a share of the real primary DiD, −0.0809 (the review computations of 14 September 2026, `notes/review_computations_2026-09-14.md`, section 5). The decomposition was repeated with the lag-τ four-vector (x_t, y_t, x_{t+τ}, y_{t+τ}) for τ = 2, 3, 5 on `ts_gsr`, windowed and global, with the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast built in the same way; the analysis plan recorded beforehand was that the lag-τ autocorrelation r_τ falls with τ and the artefact weakens (section 9).

The residual of the AR(1)-substituted estimate (called the diagnostic's residual in the record) is observed minus AR(1)-substituted sts per pair, subject, run and window, averaged over pairs; because the τ = 1 atoms depend only on the lag-0 and lag-1 correlations, the estimate depends on the AR(1) assumption only through the substitution of the two cross-lag correlations (moved from the main text's Methods). The residual DiD is positive in the four windowed variant × estimator combinations (S12 Table: +0.0115 [+0.0005, +0.0226] at the primary one); at the run level it equals the observed DiD by construction. **The cross-lag deviation and its budget.** For each pair, d is the measured lag-1 cross-correlation minus the substitution's a_y q (both directions averaged), and δ_run its mean over pairs weighted by s, the sign of the pair's run-level q, so that a positive value is a departure in the direction that lowers sts. Over the run's 14 windows of W = 60 (n_w TRs, SDs σ; π_w ∝ n_w σ_{x,w} σ_{y,w}, ψ_w ∝ n_w σ²_{y,w}, ā_y = Σ_w ψ_w a_{y,w}), δ_run is written as the sum of four terms:

δ_run = δ_within + δ_pool + δ_means + ε,   δ_within = s Σ_w π_w d_w,   δ_pool = s Σ_w π_w (a_{y,w} − ā_y) q_w,   δ_means = δ_run − δ_wd,

with δ_wd the statistic after each region's mean is removed within each window, and ε = δ_wd − δ_within − δ_pool the closing remainder, which collects the effects of window boundaries and standardisation (`notes/rev_crosslag_budget.py`, `partB12_crosslag_budget.py`, `partB13_crosslag_budget_null.py`). The identity is therefore exact by construction, δ_within and δ_pool being the two terms built independently; |ε| is 0.00002 on `ts_gsr` and 0.00005 on `ts_demean` (grand means; `notes/review_results/partB/crosslag_budget_tables.md`). For the budget the finite-sample null described above was simulated as 840-TR runs per run type, with its mean filter parameter β̄ and a two-component mixture for q solved to the data's run-level mean a, mean |q̂| and fraction |q̂| < 0.05. Six configurations were run (heterogeneity 0.5, 0.25, 1.0; placebo ACF for both runs; Gaussian q; window-level targets), ≥ 25,000 pair-runs each. Two controls, a shared slow component (λ = 0.2, a_s − a_n = 0.10) and pooling (q and filter changing at TR 240), were scored the same way. Null-corrected terms are data minus null; the reading rules were recorded before the run (record, "The cross-lag budget: pre-run entry", 16 September 2026, 16:24 UTC).

The diagnostic's one assumption, corr(x_t, y_{t+1}) = a_y q, was tested on the cross-lag correlations themselves. Their departure from it, d, lowers sts where q > 0 and raises it where q < 0 (reversing one region's sign reverses both and changes no atom). A mechanism acting through the cross-lag correlations therefore shows in δ_run, the mean of d over pairs weighted by the sign of the pair's run-level q, so that a positive value is a departure in the direction that lowers sts; a signed mean cancels on `ts_gsr`, where 54.5 % of pairs have q < 0. At the run level δ_run = +0.00340 [+0.00294, +0.00386], 14 of 14 subjects (DMT run +0.00381, placebo +0.00299): on the family's scale 44 % of the −0.0137 run-level residual at the operating point, 37 % at the pairs' run-level mean point (a 0.8666, |q| 0.1945), a scale rather than a decomposition. Four accounts leave that signature: finite sampling; pooling of windows that differ in a, q or variance under one fit; lagged coupling; and a slow component shared by the two regions, d = q(1 − λ_y)(a_s − a_n) (section 3 of this text). The last two are one account at τ = 1, every stationary lag-0/lag-1 structure being that of a VAR(1) with A = Γ₁Γ₀⁻¹; the worked example (λ = 0.25, a_s = 0.95, a_n = 0.80) is the coupled family at coefficient 0.83, c = 0.03, q_ε = 0.093 (section 3). The exact budget δ_run = δ_within + δ_pool + δ_means + ε (above) was read under rules recorded before the run, against a finite-sample null solved to the data (six configurations) and two controls. Finite sampling is a minor part: the null's δ_run is +0.00029 to +0.00046, 8–14 % of the observed δ_run (the null's value depends on the density of q̂ near zero, to which each configuration is solved; above). Of the null-corrected +0.00301 [+0.00255, +0.00347], the within-window term is most (+0.00250, 83–84 % in every configuration; a shared-slow-component control puts 90 % there, a pooling control 30 %, and in that control the within-window term equals the null's (+0.00040), i.e. it is finite sampling), pooling a minor part (+0.00046, 15 %) and the window means a minor part (+0.00004). Pooling is larger on the DMT run (DMT − placebo +0.00039 per subject, sign-flip p = 0.0006, 12 of 14; 11 % of that run's +0.00349). So the departure is present inside 2-minute windows, where pooling across windows cannot act; pooling is not its principal account, and the DMT run carries about a tenth more of it. On `ts_demean` δ_run is −0.00262 [−0.00528, +0.00006] (p = 0.053); its sign reversal without global signal regression is not explained. What acts inside the windows — common slow drive or lagged coupling, non-stationarity faster than two minutes, or a within-window finite-sample effect the null as specified does not reproduce — is not identified (S9 Table; record, "The cross-lag budget: outcome", 16 September 2026); the run-level residual itself is located by the directed and symmetric components below (B15).

The directed (antisymmetric) component (record, "The directed cross-lag component (B15)"; `partB/directed_crosslag_tables.md`, `directed_crosslag.csv`). Per pair the two cross-lag correlations depart from the substitution by d_xy = corr(x_t, y_{t+1}) − a_y q and d_yx = corr(y_t, x_{t+1}) − a_x q, with symmetric part δ_sym = (d_xy + d_yx)/2 and directed part δ_anti = (d_xy − d_yx)/2; the response of sts to each is the closed-form sts of the AR(1) matrix with only that departure added, per pair, averaged (moved from the main text's Methods). On ts_demean the directed response is −0.0085 [−0.0101, −0.0069] (14/14), the symmetric +0.0092, both +0.0008. At W = 60 the RMS of δ_anti per window is 0.110 (1/√60 = 0.129), with DiD +0.0063 [−0.0009, +0.0136], p = 0.083 (ts_gsr; B22, whose interval replaces the percentile interval of earlier drafts), against +0.0064 ± 0.0010 under a pure autocorrelation change on the band-passed generator (B17b; B24 +0.0067 ± 0.0010), and +0.0035 [−0.0036, +0.0106], p = 0.30 (ts_demean).

### The residual's response to changes in lagged structure, the aligned statistic and the directed part (moved from the main text's Results 4 on 25 September 2026)

What a change in lagged structure does to the residual depends on how the change is made (main text, Table 3). Coupling added as a VAR(1) cross-coefficient with the innovations held, the calibration's construction, also shifts each pair's lag-0 correlation and autocorrelation, and the residual falls for either sign (−0.0048 ± 0.0006 at c = +0.02 and −0.0055 ± 0.0007 at c = −0.02, at W = 60). A cross-lag change at fixed (r₁, q) raises it for either sign (+0.0098 ± 0.0009 and +0.0102 ± 0.0010 at δ = ±0.02), a change aligned with the sign of q moves it at first order (−0.0135 ± 0.0003 at δ = +0.01·sign(q), +0.0185 ± 0.0004 at −0.01·sign(q)), and a weakening of a slow component shared by the two regions, which at τ = 1 cannot be told from lagged coupling (section 3 of this text), raises it in proportion to the fall of r₁ (in population −1.70 per unit of r₁ when the shared component's autocorrelation falls and −2.89 when its weight falls; −1.59 and −2.73 per unit of pair r₁ at W = 60). The residual's rise is therefore compatible with a pure autocorrelation change that the generators do not represent, with a coupling change at fixed lag-0 structure, and with a weakening of lagged structure aligned with q.

The aligned cross-lag departure — the mean over pairs of δ_sym signed by the pair's lag-0 correlation in the other run, which carries none of the window's own selection — changed by −0.0006 [−0.0017, +0.0004] under DMT relative to placebo, against +0.0000 ± 0.0003 under a pure autocorrelation change on the band-passed generator; at the conversions of main text, Table 3 (an aligned cross-lag change, and a weakening of a shared slow component's autocorrelation) its interval admits changes accounting for between none and about half of the residual's excess over that generator's expectation, so it neither explains the excess nor excludes aligned structure as its source. On the sensitivity variant it fell, −0.0025 [−0.0047, −0.0003], an amount that at the same conversions corresponds to +0.006 to +0.007 of that variant's residual DiD of +0.018. A fall in the weight of a shared component (λ → 0.9λ in main text, Table 3) raises the residual while barely moving the aligned statistic, so that form of change is invisible to it.

At the run level the residual (−0.0137 [−0.0146, −0.0129], negative in 14 of 14) is about half the directed and half the symmetric part of the cross-lag departure (above). At W = 60 the directed part's response changed by +0.0011 [−0.0033, +0.0052] under DMT relative to placebo, an interval that contains the −0.0010 ± 0.0004 a pure autocorrelation change produces through the sampling scatter of the directed departure (B24), and the symmetric part's by +0.0103 [+0.0014, +0.0194] against +0.0037 ± 0.0009; the residual's DiD sits in its symmetric part.

Two additions were made in the revision after the second review, with rules recorded before they were run (record, "Part B, items 6–8", pre-run entry of 15 September, 07:30 UTC): the CCS definition check (above) and a split-half test of the per-subject correlation between the residual DiD and the CCS-sts DiD (`notes/partB7_splithalf.py`). In the split-half test both DiDs were recomputed on odd windows only (pre 1, 3; post 7, 9, 11, 13) and on even windows only (pre 2, 4; post 6, 8, 10, 12, 14), and the within-half correlations were compared with the cross-half ones, which shared window noise cannot produce. The rule, fixed in advance on `ts_gsr`, was: not window noise if both cross-half correlations are positive and their mean is at least half the within-half mean. In that case the two are reported together as one exploratory observation of an autocorrelation-independent component whose sign matches the original pre-specified direction, with the statement that it arose after four specification changes and requires an independent dataset. Estimation noise if the mean cross-half correlation is below half the within-half mean or either is negative; in that case both are reported as null with the test as the evidence. That rule was set without reference to the split-half reliabilities of the two quantities, and at the reliabilities observed its pass criterion lay above the largest cross-half correlation two perfectly correlated reliable components could produce, so the test could not discriminate. The flaw is recorded as such (record, correction note of 15 September) and the outcome is reported as undetermined (section 6 of this text). A third computation, the finite-sample null of the residual (`notes/review_v2_residual_null.py`), is not an addition of ours: it was written and run by the second adversarial review, before any record entry and with no recorded rule (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix B), and is reported here as a post hoc computation. In it each pair is one linear filter applied to two white noises with correlation q, so the population cross-lag correlation is exactly r₁q and every residual is finite-sample. The filter (a band-pass whose lower edge is fitted, 0.0064–0.080 Hz for the placebo function and 0.0030–0.080 Hz for the DMT-post function, times exp(−βf²)) is fitted to the pooled placebo autocorrelation function (lags 1–6 fitted 0.872, 0.549, 0.179, −0.091, −0.196, −0.168). For the DMT-post cell it is fitted to the post-injection DMT function. β and q vary across pairs so that the window-level mean pair r₁ and |q| equal the four run × period cells of `residual_source.log` (DMT and placebo, pre-injection and primary windows); 3,000 pairs × 50 windows per cell, a cell being here one of those run × period combinations. The null is stationary by construction. The third review re-ran it (it reproduces) and varied two of its free choices, the per-pair heterogeneity of the filter and whether the DMT-post autocorrelation shape is applied (`notes/adversarial_review_draft_v2_second_pass_2026-09-15.md`, Appendix, computation 3); the paper no longer quotes the range those variations give (above). One technicality of the residual's inference: its phase-randomised null randomises the observed TR-resolution series against the fixed per-window AR(1)-substituted value, so it is a null of the observed series' temporal structure rather than of the residual's construction; the sign-flip test and its inverted interval carry the residual inference.

The residual's per-subject variance is shared with CCS-sts. Across subjects the residual DiD (W = 60) correlates with the CCS-sts DiD at r = +0.799 on `ts_gsr` at W = 60, +0.094 at the `ts_gsr` global fit, +0.875 on `ts_demean` at W = 60 and +0.676 at the `ts_demean` global fit (`ccs_pub_tables.md`; moved from the main text's Results 6 and the former Fig 3c (of the drafts of 21–23 September 2026), which showed the W = 60 pair on `ts_gsr`). The split-half test (the rule above; the table below; `notes/review_results/partB/splithalf_tables.md`) was meant to separate them: on `ts_gsr` the within-half correlations are +0.847 and +0.783 (mean +0.815) and the cross-half correlations +0.160 and +0.478 (mean +0.319, ratio 0.39; after partialling out the same-half autocorrelation DiD +0.815 within against +0.154 across); on `ts_demean` +0.881 within against +0.283 across (0.32). ## 9. Manufacture and the lag variants

Where an sts-matched pair of conditions can be built (equal analytic sts, different covariance), the windowed estimator manufactures a difference at W = 60 of −9 % (F1-i), +16 % (F1-ii), +7 % ± 8 % (F2-ii) and −8 % ± 6 % (F3-b) of the real primary DiD of −0.0809, of either sign; the ± figures are the standard errors over the 4,000 windows of the two pairs whose difference is not distinguishable from zero (the review computations of 14 September 2026, `notes/review_computations_2026-09-14.md`, section 5). The first two of these pairs, F1-i and F1-ii (the asymmetric VAR(1) pairs of S16 Table: lowered autocorrelation with the cross-coupling re-solved, and raised noise correlation with the autocorrelation re-solved), have an analytic sts of 0.094 nats, an order of magnitude below the data's; for them the 840-sample fit returned +2 % and +1 % ± 1 % (S16 Table). At W = 30 it manufactures up to +33 %. At the full run length it is ≤ 2 % except in one matched pair (F2-ii, +29 %) (review computations of 14 September, section 5; a post hoc computation). A matched pair in which r₁ falls by the observed amount could not be built at data-like autocorrelation, because the analytic sts is a steep function of r₁ (≈ +0.07 nats per +0.01 near 0.87) and a weak function of q. The observed autocorrelation-function change alone, at fixed q, changes the true sts by −0.069 for a population Δr₁ of −0.010, a smaller change than the data's −0.0146, so the two are not compared directly (S13 Table); the W = 60 estimator returns −0.056 of it. This is the third figure the paper gives for the sts change that the autocorrelation change produces, beside the map projection (−0.087) and the AR(1)-substituted estimate (−0.0924) of S13 Table. The three answer three questions: the mean r₁ change passed through the slope at one point; each pair's own (a_x, a_y, q) averaged over pairs; and the change in the true sts under the whole autocorrelation-function change, before the estimator's finite-sample shrinkage. All three have the observed sign, and the differences between them are the residual and the manufacture figures seen from different sides: the diagnostic's over-shoot of the observed DiD is the residual DiD of the main text's Results 4, and the gap between the true change (−0.069) and what the estimator returns of it (−0.056) is the shrinkage characterised above. The windowed whole-brain sts sits 0.1531 nats below the global fit in the DMT pre-injection windows (1.1554 against 1.3085); the AR(1)-substituted estimate decomposes that gap into the lower within-window parameters, which the estimate from the window's own (a_x, a_y, q) captures, and a residual of −0.0489 against −0.0137 at the run level. On manufacture alone the global fit does better at 840 samples, ≤ 2 % in three of the four matched pairs but +29 % in the fourth, and its run-level residual is 1.1 % on `ts_gsr` (0.1 % on `ts_demean`); the preference for it stated in earlier drafts is withdrawn (review of 22 September, Finding 9), and the paper reports both estimators and prefers neither (main text, Results 7), for two reasons besides the fourth matched pair. First, the global fit's per-bin values are local atoms under a single run-level covariance, not per-bin decompositions. A pre/post contrast within a run — the contrast this paper runs — is therefore exposed to the within-run variance change (main text, Results 7). On the DMT run the ratio of window variance to run variance goes from 1.28 to 0.89, and the global-fit sts bins correlate with that ratio at +0.37 per run on average (review computations of 14 September, section 1). The windowed series follow the same ratio more closely — windowed sts at +0.49 and window-standardised r₁ at +0.54 per run on average, +0.77 and +0.73 on the group-mean series (review computations of 14 September, section 6) — but not by that route: each window is standardised and fitted on its own samples, so its variance level cannot enter its atoms, and the correlation records that in these runs variance falls in the windows where r₁ falls. Variance can move the windowed contrast only through the window's correlation matrix, which the diagnostic models; partialling the ratio out of the relation across the 28 group-mean windows leaves r = +0.95 (from +0.97 on the series as printed to three decimals, 0.977 unrounded, section 5 of this text; a computation of the review of 17 September 2026, `notes/fresh_review_2026-09-17/checks/check_C2_variance_ratio.log`). No contrast is residualised on the ratio: for the windowed estimator that would remove the part of the r₁ change that coincides with the variance change, not an artefact of scale. The global-fit MMI-sts contrast (−0.0801 against −0.0809 at W = 60) is therefore quoted as agreement between two estimators, not as an independent confirmation. Second, the global fit on `ts_gsr` is the one cell of four in which sts fell less than proportionally to TDMI (S8 Table: +0.0204 [+0.0032, +0.0380], p = 0.025), which is not explained here. The windowed W = 60 estimator remains the estimator of the primary contrast because it was pre-specified, and the two agree on that contrast (−0.0809 at W = 60, −0.0801 at the global fit).

S14 Table and the main text's Fig 6 give the lag variants (`ts_gsr`, W = 60; `notes/review_results/partB/lag_tables.md`). As the plan anticipated, r_τ falls with τ and the artefact weakens. At τ = 1, 2 and 3 the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast has sign-flip p = 0.0106, 0.0090 and 0.0132 (phase-randomised 0.0729, 0.0040 and 0.0020), and the sts contrast has sign-flip p = 0.0038, 0.0037 and 0.0018 (phase-randomised 0.0020, 0.0010 and 0.0010 at W = 60, and 0.0010 at the global fit); at τ = 5 the intervals of both include zero (autocorrelation contrast −0.0108, p = 0.5670; sts: W = 60 p = 0.3688, phase p = 0.2607; global fit +0.0015, p = 0.7997, phase p = 0.7692). Per subject it tracks the lag-τ autocorrelation contrast, with the sign reversed at τ = 5 because r₅ is negative and every AR(1) atom is even in the autocorrelation. At τ = 3 the tracking is only partial: the per-subject correlation is 0.626 [0.143, 0.868] (Fisher-z, B21 (d)) at W = 60 (0.885 at the global fit), while the sts contrast is −0.0043 [−0.0066, −0.0019] with the p values above. So at τ = 3 the sts contrast is only partly the r₃ contrast, which is either a limit of the map at low r_τ (r₃ = 0.14, where ∂sts/∂r is small and the estimator's scatter is a larger share of the atom) or a second remainder; the data do not say which. The lag-τ autocorrelation contrast itself grows with τ (−0.0146, −0.0468, −0.0681 at τ = 1, 2, 3; S14 Table). Relative to the sts level at each lag the sts contrast is −7 %, −24 % and −16 % at τ = 1, 2, 3 (−0.0809 of 1.1554, −0.0429 of 0.1797, −0.0043 of 0.0262; S14 Table), so in relative terms it does not weaken; and the intervals of the three autocorrelation contrasts overlap ([−0.0261, −0.0037], [−0.0799, −0.0138], [−0.1185, −0.0173]; B21's inverted intervals, `partB/inference_revision.csv`), so their growth with τ is a statement about point estimates. DMT changes the shape of the regional autocorrelation function towards a faster decay, of which the r₁ change is the lag-1 summary, and the in-band spectral centroid — the power-weighted mean frequency within 0.01–0.08 Hz, a post hoc computation (`rev_extra.py`) — rises accordingly (+0.0023 Hz [+0.0006, +0.0040], p = 0.022, 12 of 14 on `ts_gsr`; review computations of 14 September, section 1; a subject-bootstrap percentile interval, since no per-subject vector of this quantity is saved). The paper does not interpret the shape change beyond this: no pre-specified quantity addresses it, and the τ = 1 estimator the literature uses sees only its lag-1 summary. It is stated so that "DMT lowers r₁" is not taken for the whole of the effect on the autocorrelation function, and it is one candidate for why the τ = 3 sts contrast tracks r₃ only partly. No lag moves the pairs to a point where q contributes comparably, because no such point exists on the family at BOLD cross-correlations.

**Literature material moved from the main text on 23 September 2026.** Group differences in the synergy and redundancy atoms have been reported in Alzheimer's disease, schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental conditions (Down et al., 2026; Nago et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). Varley (2024) showed, for the aggregate forward synergy of a bivariate process (in ΦID terms str + stx + sty + sts), that for a pair "disintegrated" while keeping each process's autocorrelation the MMI synergy equals the self-information of the less self-predictive of the two (his Eq. 12), and on 1,000 region pairs of one human subject the synergy change on disintegration correlated with pairwise mutual information at r = 0.97. Faes et al. (2025) state the general problem — PID applied to time series rests on an implicit i.i.d. assumption that 'is typically not tested in practice and is often violated', temporal correlations have 'a profound impact' on the lag-zero decomposition, and lagged PIDs on ad hoc variables cover only 'specific portions of the system dynamics' — and propose a rate-based decomposition as the remedy (the quotations are from the arXiv version dated 7 October 2025). The ΦID authors have proposed null-model normalisation of PID atoms, naming ΦID as a possible further generalisation (Liardi et al., 2025), and, in some studies, moved towards CCS redundancy (Luppi et al., 2023). The multiscale transfer decomposition of Faes et al. (2017) is a different object from the lag-1 ΦID atoms. The surrogate gradient of Luppi et al. (2022) correlated with the six macroscale maps of their Table 1 at |ρ| ≤ 0.26, with one significant association (the glycolytic index, a PET measure of aerobic glycolysis: ρ = 0.26, spin p = 0.028), against 0.22–0.54 for the observed gradient, 0.40–0.54 for its four significant associations (cortical expansion, HAR-Brain gene expression, the glycolytic index and receptor diversity); its macaque dataset, at TR 2.6 s and not deconvolved, falls inside the table's TR range (S20 Table, row 1).

The human (HCP) data of Luppi et al. (2022): at the stated 0.008–0.09 Hz band and TR of 0.72 s the ideal-band-pass r₁ is 0.97, where ∂sts/∂r₁ = 32.8 against ∂rtr/∂r₁ = 0.06 (Table B above); its measured regional r₁ is not reported. Its primary analysis is HRF-deconvolved (Wu et al., 2013), the gradient is replicated without deconvolution, and the authors report that deconvolution 'had negligible effects on synergy and redundancy calculations' at that TR; our own evidence that deconvolution leaves the mechanism in place (S2 Text) comes from TR 2 s data and does not transfer to TR 0.72 s. Its authors recomputed synergy and redundancy on phase-randomised surrogates that keep each region's autocorrelation and remove the correlations between regions (their Table 1). On the family that surrogate sits on the q = 0 line, where sts is twice the self-information of the less autocorrelated member and rtr is zero, so the surrogate gradient is, up to noise, the regional r₁ gradient. Its correlations with the macroscale maps were small and, with one exception (the glycolytic index), not significant (main text, Discussion), so on that dataset the autocorrelation-only gradient does not reproduce the observed gradient's macroscale associations: evidence against the exposure for those associations, and the only such evidence in the table. It leaves open what this paper measures on its own data (main text, Results 3), which that study does not report. On this dataset synergy and redundancy both fell under DMT: rtr by 0.0078, a tenth of the sts fall and more than a fall in r₁ alone would give, while the AR(1)-substituted estimate from each pair's measured (a_x, a_y, q), in which |q| fell too, gives −0.0086 (main text, Table 1).
