## Timmermann et al. (2023), the source study (main text and SI Appendix)

- Methods, p. 9: "a single-blind, placebo-controlled, counter-balanced design" (who was blind is not said); a first
  visit to assess suitability; two testing days two weeks apart, each with two scanning sessions, the first task-free
  with the injection, the second with intensity ratings every minute, collected in scan runs that were not analysed.
  Stated in S1 Text and S4 Text (D3, D6, D8) and, for the counterbalanced order, in the main text's Limitations.
- Methods, p. 9: participants "laid in the scanner with their eyes closed", with an eye mask, while EEG was recorded:
  the condition, not what participants were instructed. Stated in S4 Text (A1, A5).
- Methods, p. 9: of 20 participants, four discarded for more than 20 % of the volumes scrubbed (a
  framewise-displacement threshold of 0.4) in the 8 min after the injection, and three more removed for the dynamic
  analysis at the 20 % threshold over the 28 min. Stated in S1 Text and S4 Text (D4), beside the six of twenty that
  Singleton et al. (2025) excluded; the main text's Dataset paragraph gives the six and cites Singleton et al. (2025)
  alone for them.
- Methods, p. 9: the preprocessing steps 1–11 (3dDespike, 3dTshift, 3dvolreg, BET, rigid-body registration to the
  anatomical, SyN to 2 mm MNI, scrubbing at a framewise-displacement threshold of 0.4 with the scrubbed volumes
  replaced by the mean of the surrounding volumes, 6 mm FWHM smoothing with 3dBlurInMask, 0.01–0.08 Hz band-pass with
  3dFourier, linear and quadratic detrending, nine nuisance regressors: six motion, ventricles, draining veins, local
  white matter); AFNI, FSL, ANTS and Freesurfer named, with no version of them for these images; the list ends at the
  nuisance regressors, without global signal regression (p. 8: "we chose not to perform GSR in our main analyses").
  Stated in S1 Text, in S4 Text (P1–P4 and P6) and, for the censoring, in the main text's Dataset and Limitations
  paragraphs and in S3 Text §5.
- Methods, p. 9: the exclusion criteria ("Exclusion criteria included:" age under 18, MR contraindications, "absence
  of experience with a psychedelic", an adverse reaction to one, psychiatric or physical illness, family history of
  psychotic disorder, excessive use of alcohol or drugs of abuse), so that the participants had previous psychedelic
  experience; the same list is in Singleton et al. (2025), Methods, p. 8. Stated in S4 Text (D3, D4).
- Methods, p. 9: "Values related to head motion (i.e., frame-wise displacement; FD) were significantly different
  between DMT and placebo (P = 0.003)". SI Appendix, Fig. S4 (SI p. 5): framewise displacement higher under DMT than
  under placebo in the initial 8 min, whole sample p = 0.003. Stated in the main text's Discussion ("The fall of r₁
  under DMT").
- Results, p. 4: under DMT, widespread decreases of alpha power and widespread increases of signal diversity
  (Lempel–Ziv complexity) in the EEG. Stated in the main text's Discussion ("The fall of r₁ under DMT"), where the
  work is cited beside Timmermann et al. (2019).
- Methods, p. 9: acquisition on a Siemens Magnetom Verio (syngo MR B17) with a 12-channel coil; TR 2000 ms, TE 30 ms,
  TA 28.06 min, flip angle 80°, 3.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm voxels, 35 slices, interslice distance 0 mm; a whole-brain
  T1-weighted structural scan. Stated in S4 Text (A2–A4).
- p. 2 and p. 9: 20 participants completed the study (7 female; mean age 33.5, SD 7.9). Stated in S4 Text (D5).

- p. 1: 48 patients with heart failure and 33 healthy controls; 246 regions. p. 3: of the 48 patients 23 with reduced
  and 25 with mildly reduced ejection fraction, the controls matched for age, sex and education; GE Discovery 750w 3

  two-tailed t-tests controlling for sex, age, education, diabetes, hypertension and mean framewise displacement, with

## Barnett & Seth (2011), Journal of Neuroscience Methods 201(2), 404–419 (Results 7; S3 Text §3)

- Abstract, p. 404: Granger causality for a stationary vector autoregressive process is fully invariant under an
  arbitrary invertible filter, so that filtering cannot isolate frequency-specific Granger-causal inferences; in
  practice inferences "often do change" after filtering, through the large increases in empirical model order that
  filtering induces. p. 405: the model order is allowed to be infinite. p. 406: for Gaussian processes Granger
  causality is equivalent to transfer entropy. p. 408: the filtered process has in general an infinite model order.
  Stated in S3 Text §3, as results on Granger causality regressed on the whole past; the main text's Results 7 cites
  the work for Granger causality under filtering and attributes no invariance to the lag-1 pair of transfer entropies.

## Seth, Chorley & Barnett (2013), NeuroImage 65, 540–555 (Results 7; S3 Text §3)

- Abstract, p. 540: shown "both theoretically and in a range of simulations", Granger-causal inferences are robust to
  changes in haemodynamic response properties, including the time to peak; with severe downsampling and/or excessive
  measurement noise incorrect inferences can still be drawn. p. 543: under Gaussian assumptions Granger causality is
  equivalent to transfer entropy. p. 545: the invariance applies to haemodynamic convolutions provided they are
  invertible filters, and the model order of the filtered regressions is in theory infinite. p. 548: Granger causality
  is invariant to haemodynamic convolution "(excluding confounding onset delays)". Stated in S3 Text §3; cited in the
  main text's Results 7 as the first work is.

## Luppi et al. (2022), PDF pp. 2 and 15 (Introduction; Discussion; S3 Text §10)

- Methods, p. 15: "Subtracting each region's redundancy rank from its synergy rank yielded a gradient": the gradient
  the surrogate test compares is a difference of ranks, which the Discussion and S3 Text §10 use for the attenuation
  argument. (The paragraph begins on p. 14; the sentence is at the head of p. 15.)
- p. 2: the regions are ranked by how synergistic and by how redundant their interactions are, and "the difference

  Introduction, which says that the study's gradient is of the synergy rank minus the redundancy rank.

## Luppi et al. (2023), NeuroImage 269, 119926, PDF p. 12 (Results 6; S3 Text §11; S20 Table, row 2)

- The study follows "the 'common change in surprisal' (CCS) method (Ince, 2017)" and computes its quantities "using a
  standard plug-in estimator applied to the mean-binarised BOLD signals"; it replicates its results with the Gaussian
  solver on continuous signals and with "an alternative definition of redundancy known as the minimum mutual
  information (MMI)". The Methods name the method and the estimator and do not say which sources and target were given
  to the tool, so that the two derivatives the paper quotes are those of its reading (Ince's CCS synergy of the two
  pasts about the joint future as one target), which Results 6, S3 Text §11 and S19 and S20 Tables say.
